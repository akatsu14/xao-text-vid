This was no nebulous, slow-burning discontent that had produced the decree. Its origins were exact, formed in the furnace of one intimate evening at Windsor Castle when a conversation that seemed utterly ordinary became a clear point of no return. The occasion was cloaked with the secrecy with which the palace hides its darkest wounds. There were just four of them - Queen Elizabeth, Prince Charles, Camilla and Princess Anne. No formal minutes were taken and the doors to the private drawing room were shut tight. But by the next dawn, the late Queen had summoned her confidential secretary and begun penning the same edict that would one day destroy her firstborn son.

The event was held after a family memorial ceremony, and the pall of melancholy still hung over the evening. The atmosphere was personal and solemn, and none of them anticipated a practical talk to spiral into a permanent rift. The ever-pragmatic monarch wanted to know about an issue of succession: What would happen to the royal residences after her death. She calmly repeated the old constitutional idea that the Crown’s property was not a personal estate that a king or queen could give away to a spouse or child. They were kept in trust for the institution, for the nation, forever. Camilla listened carefully, her face impassive, until she asked the question that suddenly transformed the temperature of the room. Sources close to the events of that night say she wanted to know if she would be allowed to have a private apartment permanently at Windsor and still have access to Balmoral if she outlived her husband.

To an observer the question would have seemed logical, a request for security from a woman who had devoted her life to the future King. To Queen Elizabeth, however, it seemed like a claim. The unusual, and maybe dangerous, aspect for the future King William, was that it was an attempt to carry a personal position, which existed only so long as Charles lived, onto the next reign. Anne, always the observer, watched the conflict deepen. And Charles argued his argument more vehemently, his voice rising in the fire of decades of anger. Camilla remained in charge, but the stillness after she’d asked the question grew heavier with each passing second. Then came the pivotal, and ultimately disastrous, moment. Her expression was a blank as Elizabeth read the one page. She shut the folder without signing it and pushed it back across the table When the words came, they were quiet but absolute. Anne saw the order passed. The future king, William, was later named as custodian, to ensure the order would be protected long after Elizabeth herself was gone. The process was duplicated back across the royal estate. The Royal Collection, a huge treasure house of works of art, furniture and historic objects held in trust for the crown, was an ever-present reminder that nothing was really owned, only stewarded.
