THE QUEEN'S DESPERATION AND THE SOVEREIGN'S PURSE

It is the multi-million-pound private financial engine of the Crown, a large collection of land, property and investments that provides the monarch with an income independent of the taxpayer-funded Sovereign Grant. The fund pays for the private lifestyles of the royals, supporting personal personnel, maintaining unlisted private properties like Balmoral and Sandringham, and providing allowances to members of the family who do not carry out official obligations. For ages its administration has been a matter of utmost discretion, a black box of royal finance protected from public and even parliamentary scrutiny.

King Charles III, a man long committed to the idea of a “slimmed down” and more accountable monarchy, recognised the grave threat this financial opacity faced in the 21st century. He viewed the loosely guarded system not as a privilege but as a vulnerability, a relic that allowed discretionary projects and personal vanities to bloom unchecked, fuelling a media narrative of an out-of-touch, wasteful institution. His answer was daring and alarming to some in the palace: a complete and thorough reorganisation of the household’s internal economy, with the Privy Purse at its heart. Every cent would be justified, every expense accounted for, every allowance sifted.

To steer this massive and politically sensitive shift, Charles went not to a team of accountants or a government committee, but to the one member of his family whose reputation for integrity, thrift and tireless efficiency was legendary: his younger sister, Princess Anne.
