A TITLE FORGED IN THE FIRES OF WAR AND THE DREAM OF A QUEEN

But to understand the whole weight of Prince Louis's position, we must step back in time to a period when the British monarchy was in an existential crisis. His Royal Highness, Prince Louis, was not a title that was immediately passed on to him. This was the end of a legal and political chess game that had been played out for a hundred years, beginning in the most troubled days of the First World War. In the midst of the most intense anti-German emotion ever seen in the United Kingdom, King George V took a series of unprecedented steps in 1917 to ensure his position on the throne. This was the era when German Gotha G.IV bombers were dropping death on London. His family name Saxe-Coburg and Gotha was a pointed reminder of their German background.  He dropped all his German titles and adopted a new name, which was quintessentially English: the House of Windsor. It was a hell of a public relations stroke on his behalf.

But the rebranding was more than that. George V issued a formal proclamation, called a Letters Patent, to tidy up the monarchy and free it from its great web of German relations. But the new regulation was strict: only the children of the sovereign and male-line grandchildren of the sovereign were automatically entitled to hold the title of Prince or Princess and to use the style of "His or Her Royal Highness". The decree greatly restricted who might hold these titles and use the style. No great-grandchildren were counted. By this law, which was a hundred years old, the third child of the eldest son of the Prince of Wales would not have been qualified for the place. It was a rule that had been in existence for almost a century, a hold-over from another age. But not only the globe had changed, the monarchy had too. Significantly, however, she issued her own Letters Patent in addition to that provided by her grandpa. She made a clear and forward-looking announcement: she said that all children born to the Prince of Wales's first son would be granted the title of Prince or Princess. The monarch did it in one stroke of the pen to ensure Prince George, who will one day be king, and any future siblings are equal from birth. The move brought the royal family into line with the new succession laws that ended male primogeniture. That meant that a daughter, such as Princess Charlotte, would not be forgotten by a younger brother. There's a dukedom in the wings and the ghost of Edinburgh.

As Prince Louis grows up, the question of his future employment and title is becoming a hot topic among palace strategists and royal watchers. The future of Louis, a “spare”, is more malleable than that of his brother, Prince George, who is on a clearly defined course to the throne. He might be given the Dukedom of Edinburgh, a title with the enormous weight and legacy of his great-grandfather, Prince Philip. This could be one of the biggest titles lined up for him.

The narrative of the title has a royal-strategy lesson. Originally constructed by King George I in 1726 for his grandson, it has only been used sparingly thereafter. It is reserved for senior members of the family who are expected to play a major role in public affairs. By contrast, the man who married the future Queen Elizabeth II in 1947, transforming the Dukedom of Edinburgh from an aristocratic title into an international brand that stands for responsibility, resilience and modernity, was Prince Philip. For over 70 years of his life he was the Duke of Edinburgh, a patron of science and business, the visionary founder of the Duke of Edinburgh’s Award, a symbol of unwavering devotion for the Queen. This program, now operational in over 130 countries, has made a tremendous difference in the lives of millions of youth via instilling values such as discipline, adventure and community service. It was Philip who gave the title a deep and tangible worth beyond that of a ceremonial title.

Following Philip’s death in 2021, the future of the title was a matter of contention. For a very long time, he had a strong desire for it to be handed down to his youngest son, Prince Edward. King Charles gave the Dukedom of Edinburgh to Edward on his 59th birthday in 2023, a move that was viewed as a nod to both his father’s ambition and his brother’s decades of silent service. This was a sign of respect to both these circumstances. But a life peerage was an important and strategic condition that the King inserted in his agreement. This means the title will not be handed on to Edward’s son when he dies, unlike normal hereditary dukedoms. Alternatively, it will be restored to the Crown, where it will be regranted at the monarch's discretion.

Almost all constitutional academics and royal insiders think that this was a deliberate choice, and one that was part of Charles’s longer-term vision of a leaner monarchy that is based on the direct line of succession. The title is being held in trusting care for the benefit of a succeeding generation. Prince Louis is the most suitable and reasonable contender for the future recipient. When he is a man, to give him the Dukedom of Edinburgh would be a wonderful symbolic act, directly linking him to the heritage of his mighty great-grandfather. As Philip was an indispensable supporter of the monarch this suggests that there is an expectation that he will spend his entire life in public service.

Other titles, like the Dukedom of York, are far more tricky. This title has traditionally been granted on the second son of the king . The current holder of this distinction is Prince Andrew . The public turmoil and intrigue that has surrounded him has so seriously contaminated the Dukedom of York that it is practically impossible that the King will renew it from generation to generation. Now it’s a dangerous liability. The Dukedom of Edinburgh on the other hand stands as a beacon of dignity and integrity. If and when Prince Louis is awarded it, it will be more than an honour; it will be a mission statement, a public pronouncement that he is expected to step into a role of huge responsibility, carrying forth a legacy of unshakable loyalty to the Crown and country.
