AN IMPERSONATION OF A TITLE 

To appreciate King Charles’s scheme in all its majesty, we need to recognize first that not all royal titles are created equal. In the curious and intricate world of British heraldry, a royal dukedom is the highest rank of peerage. It is a title of great importance, and has been traditionally restricted to the sons of the royal and their nearest male kin upon marriage. But among these great titles, there is one, the Dukedom of Edinburgh, which holds a place so respected that it is almost hallowed ground. It is not a title that is awarded carelessly, but one that flows directly from the sovereignty of the monarch and is intimately linked to the consort of the sovereign or the direct line of succession.

Its uniqueness is evidenced by the fact that it has a long history. Since its foundation in the 18th century, it has been held by only a few of people, and the list of its Duchesses is even more exclusive, like a roll call of the most important women in the history of the family. Princess Augusta of Saxe-Gotha became a formidable political force in her own right throughout the 1730s. She was the mother of King George III, a woman who had wielded enormous authority from behind the throne. She was the first to hold this crucial role. The title then passed to Grand Duchess Maria Alexandrovna of Russia, daughter of a Tsar, and her landing in Britain was a major geopolitical event for more than a century. More recently, Camilla, formally the Duchess of Edinburgh, married the Prince of Wales, as he was known at the time. But the title was so firmly identified with her predecessor that Camilla seldom ever used it.

But the third name on that list is the one that turns the title from a simple honor into a holy trust: Queen Elizabeth II. For the whole world, she was a queen for seventy years, and a symbol of unshakable stability and duty. But she was recognized as Her Royal Highness the Duchess of Edinburgh from the day of her wedding, which took place on November 20, 1947, and until the day she gained the throne on February 6, 1952. It was the title of her upbringing, her early marriage, her life as a navy wife in Malta and her time as a young mother to Charles and Anne, but also of her latter years. A CHANGE IN THE LAW AND IN THE LINEAGE AT THE PRESENT

It was one of the most fundamental constitutional developments in the thousand-year history of the monarchy that Princess Charlotte was able to emerge as the most prominent figure in the home. For centuries, the British Crown has adhered to a strict system of primogeniture (or more specifically, male-preference primogeniture). This old law meant that a young son may jump ahead of an elder daughter in the succession just because he was male. The legislation was to determine the fate of many royal women. Queen Elizabeth II’s own daughter, Princess Anne, was notoriously relegated to a place behind her two younger brothers, Andrew and Edward, in precedence despite being the second-born child, which curtailed her constitutional standing.

If this ancient law were still in force, then Charlotte, whose status and possible destiny is now higher, would have been pushed down to fourth in line automatically when Prince Louis was born in 2018. The monarchy , although it is believed to be immutable , must adapt to survive . " Following negotiations between Commonwealth nations at the Perth Agreement, the British Parliament passed the Succession to the Crown Act in 2013. With the approval of this historic legislation, male-preference primogeniture was legally abolished for all royals born after October 28, 2011. It was a tectonic change that put the historic institution in line with modern views about gender equality, a giant step forward.
