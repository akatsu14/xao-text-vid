To the world, Paul Hogan was Mick Dundee, the laconic, knife-wielding Australian who won over the globe with an easy grin and an outback knowledge. His image was tattooed into the communal psyche, he created an empire of laughing and red carpets, a cinematic phenomenon. But behind the big smile and the homespun humor, behind the worldwide adulation, behind the almost effortless rise to superstardom, was a private story of great sacrifice, deep regret, and the mixed and sometimes contradictory feelings of two women who loved him in ways he never quite deserved. One stuck with him through three decades of obscurity and then incredible stardom, only to be discarded. The other walked away having given up the very heart of her own identity. Paul Hogan, 86, has finally said the words, "She was the love of my life". But there's a tragic ambiguity to that statement that hangs heavy in the air: whose "she"? And what untold prices were paid for that truth to be revealed?

Way before the fabled crocodiles, and the stunning $300 million box office revenues, Paul Hogan was just a young man from Parramatta, a large neighborhood in Western Sydney. He was born on October 8, 1939, into the kind of hardscrabble working-class reality that would later become the base of his most renowned character. His hands were calloused from honest labor, his future, as he may have said, “no particular plan.” He attended Parramatta Marist High School, a Catholic school, famed for its discipline and academic difficulty, but departed the halls of the school without leaving any great scholastic imprint. The controlled setting of formal schooling did not seem to suit his restless personality or growing wit. Instead he was attracted to the concrete, demanding world of manual work, working as a rigger on the gigantic Sydney Harbour Bridge. This was a job that demanded strength, precision and an almost fearless nature, reaching the dizzying heights of one of the world’s most renowned architectural wonders. It was a life of early hours, physical effort, and the fellowship of other workers, a far cry from the sparkling stages of Hollywood. He also did lifeguard jobs at the local swimming facility in Granville to top up his income and keep his fledgling family afloat. It was there, in the chlorine-scented air and the interminable summer days of 1958, that his future, in the guise of a young woman called Noelene Edwards, finally unveiled itself.

Noelene was eighteen, Paul was nineteen. There was nothing dramatic, no huge romantic gestures in their encounter, just the basic, almost obvious connection in Paul’s own quiet words. He once summed up the origins of their relationship with his trademark bluntness: “I was a big flirt and I liked her and she liked me and we got married.” That was it. No courtly wooing, no worrying over fate. Two young teenagers in Granville, brought together by a mutual attraction, convinced in the sureness of youth that this simple mutual devotion was more than enough. They were married on June 24, 1958, and began a life together that would soon speed up. Their first child was born before Paul turned twenty and by the time he was twenty-two they already had three sons. Paul has often spoken of these early years, not with nostalgic nostalgia, nor with an inkling of self-pity, but with the pragmatic clarity of a man who just lived the life that was offered him. He had been brought up in the family, and had known no other life, and missed nothing. They eked out a living on what the labour on the bridge and the seasonal variations at the pool could bring. And Noelene, the silent glue holding together their expanding household, took care of the children, the money, the ordinary ebb and flow of their simple lives. For his part, Paul kept coming to work, day after day, year after year. They were, in the most basic and profound sense, making something from nothing, brick by meticulous brick, an act of tenacity and common dedication.

And then, one day in 1971, an ordinary evening, Paul Hogan, the rigger and lifeguard, did something that would change the course of his life forever and, by implication, the lives of those closest to him. He stepped onto the set of a prominent Australian talent show called New Faces. But he didn't look like a hopeful contestant seriously wanting to show off a talent. Rather, it was a premeditated act of humorous subversion, a daring plot devised through his close examination of the show’s structure. He'd figured out that a lot of the fun of the program was the judges' often vicious shaming of the amateur performers. Paul, with an impish twinkle in his eye, and an intuitive grasp of audience psychology, decided to turn the tables. Instead he would go out and disgrace the judges. He was wearing his work boots, a badge of his blue-collar bona fides, and he rattled off a string of sharp, disrespectful jokes at their expense, punctuating his routine by hitting two shovels together with a theatrical clang. The audience, charmed by his bravery, roared with laughter and applause. He was asked back time after again, and his section was a feature of the show. His anti-establishment style of comedy, with a down-to-earth appeal, drew the notice of Mike Willesee, notable journalist and host of current events program, A Current Affair. The unlikely start to Paul Hogan's television career was this serendipitous meeting, formed out of a rebellious humorous drive.

New Faces was such a success that it quickly led to The Paul Hogan Show, a television series that ran for 60 episodes between 1973 until 1984. It became a cultural phenomenon in Australia and was hugely popular in the United Kingdom. It was based on the same principle that had so powerfully appealed on New Faces: the ordinary man, simple and unimpressed, refusing to be awed by anyone who thought himself important or deserving deference. Hogan’s humour was sharp, insightful and very much embedded in the Australian psyche, a welcome antidote to perceived pomposity. But despite his apparent comfort in stepping into the limelight, Paul said the change from bridge worker to TV personality was the hardest thing he ever undertook. He said it was harder than dealing with the maze of Hollywood, harder than contending with the soaring heights of international renown. It was because of a peculiar soreness he had never expected, he explained. “I was poor and famous,” he said, in a famous, dizzying dichotomy of early notoriety. And that was uncomfortable.” This period, he insisted, was the real crucible; everything that followed, by comparison, was easy.

Through all the upheaval of their increasing celebrity, the dizzying shift in their lives, Noelene remained at home. She was the rock-solid constant, raising their five children, running the household and seeing her husband's metamorphosis into a national idol from the inside. This was a perspective peculiar to the wives of renowned men, a vantage point from which the exterior splendor and devotion were invisible, and what was felt was the mounting absence, the rising distance, and the quiet weight of unshared memories. Noelene was not a lady who craved the limelight; her strength was her quiet endurance, her devotion to the family they had made. But the pressures of fame, the unrelenting demands of Paul’s new work and the growing divide between their two worlds started to take their toll. After 23 years of marriage, the strain proved too much and they divorced for the first time in 1981. Paul has never given any complicated justifications for this first separation. He is, after all, blunt. There were the pressures of celebrity, the widening physical and emotional gulf, the seismic shift in dynamics that occurs when one person begins to spend their time on the other side of the planet, beloved by millions of strangers. And these were the silent builders of their undoing. Whatever the precise triggers, the marriage which had begun so innocently by a swimming pool in Granville gently, almost inexorably, fell apart, mercifully without the public ugliness that would eventually mark their final split.

And they reunited in a twist that only made the ultimate pain of their tragedy worse. Paul Hogan and Noelene Edwards were remarried less than a year after their divorce in 1982. It was an act not of naive hope, but possibly of deeper insight, an awareness that beneath all the hardships, and under the disruptive forces of celebrity, thirty years of shared history, of forging a life from start, could not just be put away or discarded. It was a sign of the strength of the original link, a desperate attempt to get back what had been lost, to heal the rift and return to the comfort of the known past. For a fleeting, optimistic moment it looked like their narrative may, after all, have a different ending, a modest triumph of lasting love over the corrosive effects of stardom.

But it did not. In 1985 Paul Hogan began a project that would define his profession and change his personal life permanently, once again. He ventured far into the Australian Outback to make a film that he had co-written, and risked everything, financially and professionally. It was on this distant set, in the harsh beauty and merciless nature of the Australian interior, that something happened, something that Noelene had possibly already felt coming, an unavoidable consequence of the life they had chosen, or rather, the life that had chosen Paul.

Linda Kozlowski was born July 7, 1958, in Fairfield, Connecticut, a world away from the sandy plains of the outback. She was a Juilliard-trained actress who had paid her dues on the grueling stages of Broadway, a witness to her genuine dedication to her craft. She had only two screen credits when she auditioned for Crocodile Dundee, but she had a tremendous endorsement: a call from Dustin Hoffman. She worked with the distinguished actor on his Broadway revival of Death of a Salesman and again on the 1985 film adaption. When the Dundee crew began casting, Hoffman called personally to put in a good word for her. "Dustin's call was like the Good Housekeeping Seal of Approval — but Linda got the movie because they liked her," one of Linda's friends told People magazine at the time. The call clearly opened the door, offering a crucial introduction. It was in the audition room, however, with her talent and presence, that she won the part.

Linda came to Australia ready for the hard job of film-making, but perhaps not quite ready for the wild and rugged circumstances of the outback. Her lodgings, a little cabin, stood on the very edge of a crocodile-infested swamp. These old predators, safe by law and emboldened by their unquestioned supremacy, had grown fat and brave and regularly came on land at night, their eyes shining in the dark. During the aquatic shots, a crew member stood with a loaded Magnum between Linda and the water as a harsh reminder of the ever-present risk, a quiet guardian against the lurking monster. But during all this Linda Kozlowski never had one complaint. Paul Hogan knew that, and he told People magazine that he thought her persistence would be short-lived. "We thought the bugs and the snakes and the danger of crocodiles would freak her right out. There have been three or four deaths up there from crocodiles since Christmas. But she never complained. "He added, with the bluntness that had always been his hallmark, a note of grudging admiration: "Before Dundee she was unemployed and broke, but she delivered the goods. She was a star-in-waiting.

The director, Peter Faiman, a seasoned watcher of human dynamics, watched the growing bond between his two stars with a professional eye, knowing something significant, something beyond the necessities of the job, was happening. “They got to know each other better and better and better as the movie went on,” he said. Paul’s close friend Delvene Delaney was even more blunt in her appraisal of the unmistakable chemistry and growing intimacy. “Linda came to me at one point and she was worried about it because she knew Paul was married and he had five kids,” Delaney revealed. “But it was irresistible.” The attraction, the connection, the emotional and physical pull between Paul Hogan and Linda Kozlowski was a force of nature, as wild and untamed as the outback itself. It was a connection that would wash away the carefully crafted existence Paul had built, leaving a path of severe personal upheaval in its wake.

The movie is Crocodile Dundee and it was released in 1986 and became an instant worldwide phenomenon, one of the biggest comedy successes in the history of film. It made an incredible $300 million globally off a fraction of that budget, turning Paul Hogan overnight from an Australian national treasure into an international superstar. It also pushed Linda Kozlowski’s career from relative obscurity to a Golden Globe nomination, abruptly into the stratosphere. But this sudden triumph came at a very high personal price. Noelene Edwards’ second marriage to Paul Hogan was beyond saving. And this time the finish was anything but tranquil.

It was, the Australian press said, one of the ugliest celebrity splits the country has seen, played out in the brutal spotlight of a ravenous media. It unfolded in the newspapers, on the talk shows, on the airwaves, a public tragedy of pain and betrayal. Fans who had come to identify with Paul Hogan and the everyman image he projected, took sides publicly, creating a split narrative around the marriage. Noelene, a lady who had never sought the spotlight, who had always enjoyed the peaceful dignity of a private existence, suddenly found herself thrown into the harsh heart of a media storm, with nowhere to flee, nowhere to hide from the relentless scrutiny.  But through it all, she took the enormous pressure with a surprising dignity, a calm strength that belied the sensationalism that around her. In the midst of the maelstrom she said one profound sentence, clear and undeceived and calm: "I had the best years of his life." Not bitterness, not anger, but the quiet, almost stoic understanding of a woman who knew precisely what she had possessed, and exactly what had been irrevocably taken from her when Paul chose somebody else. Later it would be revealed that the break was absolute, the wound so deep that after that last divorce, Paul did not speak to Noelene for seventeen long years. Seventeen years of silence between the guy she'd married at nineteen, the lady who'd had and raised his five kids, the partner who'd stuck by him when all he was was a rigger on a bridge with nothing to offer except himself.

With the years Noelene found her own serenity, a peaceful happiness apart from the roar of celebrity. In 2000, she married businessman Reg Stretton in a wedding at the Manly Pacific Parkroyal Hotel in Sydney. Seventeen years they had had, a truly happy and amazingly calm life, the kind of life she had always been made for, away from the prying lenses of cameras and the dramatic headlines. Their peaceful union lasted until Reg’s death in 2017, a tribute to a love, built on mutual respect and common ideals, a stark contrast to the stormy existence she had earlier experienced.

Paul, meanwhile, married Linda Kozlowski on May 5, 1990 in his hometown of Sydney. He was fifty-one, she thirty-two years old. The world had been waiting for this wedding, starving for the special love story that started with scandal and ended, as they saw it, with a fairy tale. The admiring masses, and the love tale finishing itself, it was the perfect climax. But many who knew them understood this to be the beginning of something far more complicated, a partnership founded on passion but also haunted by the choices that had led them there.

In the early years of their marriage there was real warmth, and certainly no lack of public affection. Paul genuinely adored Linda, and was often effusive in his words. "She's absolutely gorgeous. She knocks my socks off - she really does," he told Woman's Day magazine. They went onto other film projects, trying to cash in on the Crocodile Dundee craze. Crocodile Dundee II, from 1988, was a decent $239 million worldwide, but there was a definite sense from critics that the novelty was starting to wear thin, the magic a little lessened. Then came 1990’s Almost an Angel, a comedy where Paul plays a burglar who thinks he has become an angel after a near death experience. It was a total failure, grossing just $1.6 million in its opening weekend domestically. It seemed the public didn't want Paul Hogan as an angel, it wanted him as Mick Dundee with a knife and a hat and the wild untamed outback behind him.

The media was along for the ride every step of the way during these years, with the relentless scrutiny of fame at that level. Take, for example, a 1988 report in The Sydney Morning Herald of a transatlantic flight to London on which, according to a fellow passenger, Paul and Linda "hardly left each other alone through the whole flight". In those early, heady years, their romance was just what the world had decided it was: electric, unapologetic and undeniably the real thing, a passionate love affair that seemed to defy convention.

What the world saw on the outside, the dazzling façade of a recognized couple, was not the complete narrative that was going on within their inner reality. Linda came into the marriage with a profession that Hollywood, despite her Juilliard training and breakout success, would never quite allow her to build on her own terms. Following the success of the first Crocodile Dundee she deliberately rejected several roles she felt were beneath her and refused to be pigeonholed. “After Crocodile Dundee I was offered a lot of things,” she said. “Most of them were playing the girlfriend of some funny man.” Despite her best efforts to carve her own career, opportunities for meaty, demanding roles were hard to come by. She tried anyhow, with a string of direct-to-video films through the 1990s, titles largely forgotten now. She felt that the event ate away at her spirit and artistic integrity. "These schlocky straight-to-video pictures were causing me an ulcer," she said, "since I was literally the only one on the set that cared about anything.

Finally, the disillusionment was too great to ignore. She gave up acting completely. I thought, this isn’t fun anymore. This isn’t why I studied, this isn’t what I love.” A heart-wrenching acknowledgement of a dream deferred. In 1998 they had a son, Chance, and for a while the choice to give up her work to be a mother seemed enough. A time of apparent fulfilment, she told the Scripps Howard News Service in 2001: "I'm 43 years old, I've got a baby, I'm happy and content".

But contentment on the back of sacrifice frequently has a limited shelf life, a period of time before the sacrifices on which it is based start to corrode its foundations. Paul was a man of the world after all but he was also very traditional in his expectations of wife and family life, in ways that Linda's suppressed ambitions could no longer allow. He wanted stability, a home-centered life, a mate who put family first. That was what Linda had given him, her career dreams, the ultimate sacrifice, out of her deep love for him and their baby. But in giving it she had lost, in a very real way, a part of herself, something that could never be restored. Later friends of the couple would say that she was beginning to feel more and more like a prisoner, that she had ceased to exist as a person and had become only "Mrs. Hogan," her identity swallowed up in his renown.

In October 2013, after twenty-three years of marriage, Linda Kozlowski filed for divorce in the Los Angeles Superior Court, citing the ever-popular legal catch-all, “irreconcilable differences.” (A term that covers a thousand distinct, unidentified wounds.) Their son, Chance, was fifteen then, old enough to feel the vibrations of their split. The deal provided Linda with a $6.25 million lump sum payout and the right to live in their Los Angeles house for four years or until she remarried. Importantly, Paul kept the ownership of the Crocodile Dundee persona - the same character that had united them. The woman who had helped him bring the character to life left the building, attempting to get her own back.

Subsequently, both sides issued public remarks that sounded friendly on the surface. But Linda was talking with a new lucidity, a real sense of release. “I have been living in Paul’s shadow for many, many years and it’s nice to have my own light at the moment,” she told New Idea magazine. Paul responded to The Daily Telegraph with the self-aware deflection and hint of his trademark humour that he would often use as armour to hide the deeper feelings. "I'm very flighty. A woman lasts for about a quarter of a century and then they get bored with me," he quipped. "We were opposites in everything. From the food we ate to the music we liked, the entertainment we liked, the colours, the clothes, the places, everything. It worked anyway."

But beneath the contrived calm was a more complicated picture, and the public story was one of an amicable breakup. Linda went to Morocco to find herself. There she met a tour guide called Moulay Hafid Baba and writes of an instant, deep connection, as if they’d known each other for a millennia. In 2017 they married, a fresh chapter in a far away country. Paul's manager issued a statement saying he was "genuinely delighted" when news of Linda's marriage reached him and that any suggestion otherwise was "pure fabricated invention". But a source close to Paul told New Idea a more poignant story. "Paul is devastated that Linda has sold her home and cut ties with America. He's going to be incredibly lonely now that they are moving to Morocco. Paul doesn't have a lot of friends in L.A. and finds it hard to get close to new people, so it's affecting him a lot." Linda, meanwhile, is still reportedly concerned for her ex-husband, particularly his ongoing smoking. “She is always nagging him to go for regular health check-ups and to give up the cigarettes, because she wants him around for a long while yet,” a friend told Women’s Day. “It was a strange kind of tenderness, this enduring concern, the woman who had to leave to find herself, still watching from a distance, making sure he didn’t disappear altogether.”

And Paul Hogan’s twilight years have not been softened by any amount of fame, fortune or public affection. In 2003, the Australian Taxation Office initiated a tough inquiry following claims he had siphoned millions of dollars from his film earnings into offshore accounts. The probe, dubbed Operation Wickenby, was a dogged, lengthy operation that took seven agonizing years and threw a long, dark shadow over his life and reputation. So in 2010, when Paul returned to Australia for his mother’s funeral, he was served with a departure prohibition order, barring him from leaving the country until he paid the alleged tax debt of a staggering $37.5 million Australian dollars, at a deeply personal and vulnerable time. It was a public disgrace, a clear reminder that even worldwide superstars are not above the long arm of the law. The accusations were later dismissed and a confidential settlement was reached in 2012, but the trauma left an indelible impact, a period of enormous stress and uncertainty, chipping away at his peace of mind.

The physical toll of a full, possibly wild life became apparent at 80. His tough reputation was tarnished by muscle loss and suffering. He seemed weaker than Mick Dundee on his rare public appearances. Never one to whine, he discussed these physical issues. Introspection has permeated his speech at calmer, more deliberate moments. All at once. He thought, "Fame, fortune, and a great wife," realizing the temporariness of success and the pain of loss. But I still feel like I missed something most important "It was a rare peek into public life's emotional cost and buried regrets.

Then December 2024. A sad full-circle scenario that juxtaposed the past and present felt forced. For the first time since their divorce, Paul and Linda appeared together in Crocodile Dundee: The Encore Cut, a digitally restored 4K version with a new documentary. Paul, 85 and frail, stood again next to Linda for the cameras, a shell of his former self next to the woman who had shared so much of his life. He watched the recreated footage of the two of them in 1986—young, lively, thrilling, and unstoppable—and smiled to hide his genuine sentiments. The audience chuckled when he said that the restoration improved him. Lifelong humoring deeper feelings when honesty would be too costly. He always did.

The character, not the man, owns his happiest, painless experiences. Clint Eastwood leaned over from two tables away, took his table knife, and softly held it up at a supper with Elizabeth, honoring Dundee's famous blade. Paul understood immediately. Eastwood looked at him and said, ‘Sir, you’re right.’ He remembers sitting between Prince Charles and Princess Diana at the premiere of Crocodile Dundee II, feeling them touch his elbow when something in the film made them laugh, and thinking, with his usual humility and incredulity, ‘Don’t they know I’m a rigger from Parramatta Paul Hogan can describe the character's achievements and validations for free. Life-changing personal ones cost him more.

Paul Hogan's calm retirement was disturbed by marriage in May 2026. Police attended his Venice Beach house after a family visit. Domestic abuse led to his arrest a few blocks away. He lives with Chance, his twenty-seven-year-old son with Linda. At eighty-six, Paul addressed cops at his front door with a fatherly mix of warmth and annoyance. He'd spoken about Chance before, with the affection and frustration of absent fathers. After calling his child a “terrible person” for posting contentious content on social media, he softened with protective tenderness: “He knows they’re watching him and he puts on something for them.” Perhaps Dad's most honest and saddest statement regarding his son.

Paul Hogan, 86, is in that Venice Beach estate, miles away from his kin. Noelene has enjoyed the happiest years of his life in Sydney, secretly building a life of his own, finding pleasure, including an Instagram account filled with Italian beachside images. Linda, who found her light, spends three nights in the Sahara in Marrakesh with a man who makes her laugh and creates something separate from Hollywood and Paul’s legacy. Paul, the Parramatta rigger and global star, walks alone on Venice Beach where he made his name and stayed long after his reasons for staying passed on. “She was the only one for him,” he said. Likely Linda, the strong love that transformed his professional and personal life.  Those who have followed his extraordinary, winding journey know what he may not have fully acknowledged: Noelene Edwards was his lifetime love, a calmer, deeper love that existed before fame and remains when it fades.
