In a town where persona often outweighed the men who played them, Lee Majors was an ideal of resilience and subtle strength. For decades he was the man who could not be slain, rebuilt or slowed down — at least on film. His bionic stride rang through seventy countries and his visage was a familiar and reassuring presence in millions of homes. But under the indomitable hero was a guy with a deep, hidden wound, a story of love and betrayal and a bond that lasted that he told almost no one about. Now 87, with a lifetime of experience in the public eye, and the quiet dignity of a man who has seen it all, Lee Majors has finally spoken the truth about the woman he could never forget, and the one, seemingly innocuous request that changed his life forever. But to comprehend the depth of what he lost, you must first appreciate the hard road that made the man, the fundamental foundations on which a life worth such a priceless loss was painstakingly constructed.

Harvey Lee Yeary’s beginning was a stark, harsh nothingness. He was born on April 23, 1939, in the small industrial city of Wyandotte, Mich., under tragic circumstances. Lee's father was a steel mill worker who died in a tragic accident before Lee was born. His life had just started and then another painful blow fell upon the newborn when his mother was killed by a drunk motorist at just eighteen months old. An orphaned youngster at the early age of two. A child that has lost so much in this world already. His aunt and uncle, who lived in a small hamlet in the foothills of the Appalachians called Middlesboro, Kentucky, adopted him. They gave him a home, stability, affection, showered him with everything a child could possibly want, except for one critical thing: the entire, unadulterated truth about who he was. Perhaps to spare him sorrow, this omission had the unintended effect of preparing him for a major personal discovery many years later.

But truth, as it frequently does, found its way to the top. As a youngster, amid the stillness of his adoptive home, Lee found a neglected box in the attic. Inside was a stack of ancient newspaper clippings, yellowed with age, each one a piece of a past he had never known. The headlines shouted of his parents’ tragic deaths, creating a stark picture of the orphaned youngster he had once been. For many, such a revelation, so impersonal, so unexpected, would have been shattering, would have led to a terrible unraveling. But even so Lee Majors had a tremendous inner tenacity. He would not sink to despair, but he made a quiet, firm decision: he would not give the people who had chosen him, who had opened their hearts and home, one reason to regret their tremendous gesture of compassion. This oath, sprung from the fires of teenage revelation, became a tenet, an unspoken promise to live a life worthy of their sacrifice.

His early years at Middlesboro were characterized by an almost compulsive pursuit of athletics. He choose the name Lee Majors, a nod to his football hero Johnny Majors, a telling sign of his goals. A star athlete at Middlesboro High School, he had the innate ability and drive to land a coveted football scholarship to Indiana University. The dream of a professional athletic career looked close at hand. But fate, with its merciless twists, had other plans. But a career-ending back injury abruptly cut his promising football career short, and he was left to painfully re-evaluate his future. Now the road he had so carefully laid out was abruptly blocked and he was at a crossroads.

Undeterred, with the resilience that would become his trademark, Lee Majors pivoted. He moved to the expansive, sun-drenched city of Los Angeles, a place linked with reinvention and the pursuit of dreams. He sought acting classes here, a fresh venue for his quiet intensity and natural charisma to find an expression. He was twenty-five, a late start for an ambitious actor, and his odds were against him. Still, in a move that attested to his blossoming talent and obvious screen presence, he beat out more than four hundred aspirants, including a young Burt Reynolds, for the important role of Heath Barkley in The Big Valley. He starred with the renowned Barbara Stanwyck for four seasons, an indomitable mentor whose counsel was vital. Stanwyck, a titan of Hollywood’s golden age, gave Majors three simple, but profound, pieces of advice that he’d carry with him for the rest of his career, repeating them for fifty years because they were the ones that truly held: “Be on time, know your lines, hit your mark and keep your mouth shut.” These words, embodying professionalism and humility, became the bedrock of his approach to his craft.

His private life also was in a state of flux at the time. His first marriage, to Eastern Kentucky University classmate Kathy Robinson, lasted until 1964, shortly after his relocation to Los Angeles to pursue an acting career. They had a son, Lee Majors II. The distance plus the tremendous financial demands of a young actor trying to get established out of nothing tested their young marriage, and they just couldn't handle the strain. Lee remained in California dedicated to his developing profession, while Kathy returned to Kentucky with their son. Sadly, that physical isolation turned into an emotional one, creating a distance between him and his firstborn that, by his own modest admission, never entirely closed. A subject he had rarely talked of publicly, a silence that, in itself, speaks volumes about the underlying grief and regret he carried.

Meanwhile, Lee Majors was getting a taste of Hollywood’s social scene, a place that was turning him into a famous face throughout the mid-sixties while The Big Valley slowly built his career. He dated actress Patti Chandler for about three years, and all sources find him attentive and generous throughout the relationship. Chandler even made a cameo appearance on an episode of The Big Valley, and Majors, with typical insouciance, kept her pet boa constrictor at his ranch in California. This unusual detail spoke volumes about his easy-going personality. That relationship, like so many in the fleeting realm of entertainment, ran its run. There was also a short affair with Sally Field. Neither said anything explicit about their time together, but Majors, with a self-deprecating grin that typically hid deeper feelings, gave the game away years later. In 1981, when he recorded the theme song for his hit series The Fall Guy, he sang the line, "I've been on fire with Sally Field." It was a moment of subtle humor, a classy nod to a previous chapter, delivered with the knowledge that some truths are best shared with a wink and a smile. Sally Field, meantime, married Steven Craig in 1968. It was the same year that Lee Majors was about to meet the woman who would define not only the next chapter of his life, but in many significant ways, every chapter that followed.

Often in a business where personal and professional lives were interwoven, his representative arranged the meeting to Farrah Fawcett. His agency showed him photos of his latest female clients and told him to choose the prettiest. Lee decided instantly, and without a doubt. The agent made the call. Her name was Farrah Fawcett, a twenty-one year old ingenue from Corpus Christi, Texas, new to the city. She didn’t have an acting resume, didn’t have contacts, but had a beauty that was so otherworldly it could stop a conversation in mid-sentence. Their first date, nevertheless, was everything but pleasant. Farrah, wanting to sound sophisticated, ordered a scotch and coke, a drink she knew little about. Predictable and speedy, she fell ill fast. Lee sat across from her, wondering whether her discomfort was real dislike of him, or just a bad reaction to the drink. Not exactly the auspicious start one could expect for a future renowned pair.

But Lee Majors, ever the gentleman, was not put off. The next morning he delivered thirteen yellow roses, a lovely, forgiving gesture. Farrah was so moved by the sweet gesture she said yes to a second date. Years later she'd tell People magazine about the moment he came to pick her up, saying she "melted into a thousand pieces." It was love at first sight, she asserted. Maybe a feeling that will likely come after a fairly unglamorous first date when someone gives thirteen yellow roses. In each other they found a common ground, a bond based on their Southern roots and their aspirational journey to Los Angeles. Both had arrived in the city of dreams with hopes that their family back home probably found a bit baffling. Farrah sweetly dubbed him “big grump-head,” in a teasing reference to his normally quiet disposition. In those early days, when her career was just beginning, she had a more typical job, ensuring she got home in time to make dinner. She characterized herself as “a very compliant person who just wanted to cook his meals, clean his house and be dependent.” This was the woman Lee Majors married in 1973, a lady whose domesticity and desire to be reliant seemed to mesh perfectly with his own growing success and security. What he could never have guessed, however, was that the industry he was going to assist her join, the doors he would so effortlessly open, would change her so thoroughly that the woman who eventually walked out of his life was hardly the same one he had known.

But prior to the tectonic shifts in their personal lives, 1973 also saw a different sort of phenomenon: the part that would launch Lee Majors to global superstardom. He was cast as Colonel Steve Austin in The Six Million Dollar Man, an astronaut who is reconstructed with bionic implants after a near-fatal accident and given incredible strength, speed and eyesight. The show was an instant, enormous hit, transmitted in over seventy countries, and made Lee Majors a household name across continents. His likeness was on action figures, lunchboxes and posters, seemingly ubiquitous across America. The cultural impact of the show is everywhere, as children everywhere imitated his distinctive slow-motion run on playgrounds. The day was five years ago, and Lee Majors was without exaggeration one of the most famous men on planet.

What made The Six Million Dollar Man different from many of its peers was its nuanced characterization of its hero. Steve Austin was not only a formidable, unstoppable force. He was a man that was fighting great inner strife. He had been rebuilt without his consent, living with the crushing weight of what had been done to him, bearing his remarkable powers not as an unqualified gift, but more like a burden. In his nature, Lee Majors was a quiet man, and he was the one actor who could bring that internal conflict to life. He gave the character a depth and a gravitas that made it more than just another science fiction adventure. The performance resonated, and Steve Austin became a hero who was aspirational and also immensely human.

The success of The Six Million Dollar Man resulted in a spin-off, The Bionic Woman, which debuted in 1976 and aired at the same time as its predecessor until both were discontinued in 1978. Austin's love interest, Jaime Sommers (played by Lindsay Wagner), had got similar bionic modifications following her own near-fatal skydiving accident. The two shows often featured the same characters and often had episodes that crossed over to each other and delighted the audience. Majors and Wagner clearly had chemistry, but by all accounts, it was strictly professional—and surprisingly efficient. Wagner later praised Lee Majors as one of the most generous performers she had ever worked with, citing his persistent attention on making the scene work as a whole, rather than just on improving his personal performance. That largeness of heart mirrored his natural nature as well as it marked his professional conduct.

As Farrah’s dreams began to grow, Lee Majors was riding a crest of great personal and professional success and was able to use his standing in the industry to help promote her budding ambitions. He got her a guest spot on his own series, Owen Marshall, Counselor at Law, giving her a prominent credit when her resume was still thin. He was, for all intents and purposes, really and whole-heartedly supportive of her ambitions, aggressively pushing her towards opportunities and unlocking doors that may otherwise have stayed shut. He was a cheerleader for her when she landed the role of Jill Munroe on Charlie’s Angels in 1976, even as it was becoming increasingly evident that the show was about to do something remarkable with her public image.

But he could not have predicted the sheer size of the event that was going to overwhelm their life. Charlie's Angels wasn't just a success it was a cultural phenomenon and Farrah Fawcett wasn't just a star in it. She was the face of an era, an icon whose image went beyond the show itself. The next poster—Farrah in a sexy red one-piece bathing suit, her blond hair blowing in the breeze, her grin shining—sold a staggering twelve million copies, becoming one of the most printed images of the twentieth century. It was a moment of unparalleled stardom, a visual icon for a whole generation. What very few people knew, and what Lee Majors has since spoken about with a complex mixture of pride and something harder to explain, is that he was the one who directly selected that iconic shot. “I went with the poster,” he told People magazine. “The famous one. I picked the picture.” Looking back, it seemed to have an almost prophetic weight, for in a way he chose the image that would define his wife’s global reputation, and in doing so, had unintentionally unleashed forces that would reshape their shared reality.

Fame, an ever-flowing tide, started to change the fundamental fabric of their marriage in ways neither had anticipated. Their hitherto synchronized schedules fell completely out of whack. He was putting in killer 14-hour days on the set of The Six Million Dollar Man, and she was putting in the same kind of killer 14-hour days on the set of Charlie's Angels. Days progressed into weeks and then months, the gap between them growing all the while. “There was a year or so when I think I saw her two weeks in one year,” Lee Majors told People in his seventies in a hushed, almost incredulous tone, looking back on that time. Two weeks out of a whole year. How could such a tenuous link be called a marriage? it was rather two people sharing a mailing address, their lives diverging under the crushing force of their own stars.

Lee pushed Farrah to leave Charlie’s Angels after one season to explore feature films in an attempt to guide her career toward what he thought would be more meaningful and artistically satisfying parts. He believed in her potential, and he wanted the business to see her as a real actor, not a pretty face on a poster. But this decision was not without a heavy price. She was sued by producers of the show for breach of contract for seven million dollars, which gives you an idea of just how valuable she was for the show. Her next two major pictures, Somebody Killed Her Husband and Sunburn, were critical and commercial flops, despite the fact she was commanding $750,000 a picture. Lee had opened the door and she'd walked through it, but the room on the other side wasn't what they both had imagined. He confessed in one of his more contemplative moods, "There are times when I think that maybe I created a monster. But then, deep down, I know that's just not true." It was a feeling born of frustration and helplessness, an acknowledgement of the unforeseen consequences of their shared journey, rather than a genuine accusation.

By 1979 the split of their lives was in fact unavoidable, a slow, painful drift that felt out of their control. What happened in Toronto, however, what took a painful but perhaps bearable conclusion and made it into something that left an indelible mark that Lee Majors has carried with him ever since, was not inevitable. He was in the Canadian city in the fall of 1979, filming The Last Chase, a project that gave him a little break from the storm of his private life. It was there he bumped across an old buddy from the freewheeling sixties, Ryan O’Neal, when Ryan was starring in Peyton Place and Lee was establishing his name in The Big Valley. Two young actors who had caroused together in their bachelor days, were reconnecting after years apart, their careers having taken wildly different, albeit equally visible, trajectories. Their friendship, founded on common adolescent experiences, was taken up again with an easy familiarity. By the time Ryan had to go back to Los Angeles, Lee sensed a new companionship, a link repaired. And then he made the request he has had to live with, and think about, ever since.

In O'Neal's later memoir, Lee informed Ryan, "She's all alone up there," referring to Farrah, who was also in Los Angeles. 'Why not take her out to supper one night?' It was a token of solicitude, a simple act of kindness, a husband leaving his wife's temporary company in the charge of a friend of his own acquaintance. Ryan O’Neal, he says, was reluctant at first. He could feel new feelings stirring inside him, and he knew immediately that they were wrong. He fought the impulse to call for a week. Then the opportunity came—a concert by a performer Farrah admired. It was an excellent excuse. And the story continued with the unrelenting sad momentum these sorts of stories seemed to have, a force neither person felt able or ready to stop. Lee Majors didn't learn the truth from Farrah, nor from Ryan, but like he learnt most things about his own marriage in that turbulent time: from the outside, from whispers and observations, from the subtle, irrefutable change in their dynamic. When the reality could no longer be ignored he went straight for Ryan. He called him up and told him to back off and respect the bounds of their friendship and his marriage. Ryan's answer was straightforward, stark and definitive. "I can't. I really love her."

When People magazine printed a story on the whole situation in 1980, Lee Majors issued a statement that, in its unvarnished truthfulness, was a breath of fresh air compared to the carefully crafted statements that are a Hollywood staple. It was a moment of uncommon, unadulterated vulnerability, a feeling he has rarely equalled since, or before. "I joke and try to be funny all the time," he admitted. "But the truth is I'm all alone and I'm miserable without her." There was no pretense in that statement, no attempt to win sympathy or influence public opinion. It was the truth, plain and simple, expressed with a calm pain that lasted long after the words were read. The divorce was completed on February 16, 1982. Lee Majors managed the traumatic breakup of his marriage with amazing grace and an almost complete absence of public drama. He didn’t write a tell-all memoir, and he didn’t publish a public statement trying to paint himself as the victim. Instead he did what he always did. He went to work.

But before the divorce was official, he was already working on production on The Fall Guy in 1981. In this new series, he played a Hollywood stuntman, Colt Seavers, who moonlighted as a bounty hunter. Majors brought the same physical comfort, genuine charm, and grounded realism to the character that had made Steve Austin so fascinating a decade earlier. And in a typical demonstration of his dry wit and self-awareness, he also sang the theme tune himself, "The Unknown Stuntman", an intentionally self-deprecating ditty that became one of the most recognizable television themes of the decade. The lyrics, with their ironic acknowledgement of a professional actor dealing with the highs and lows of fame, effectively summed up his personality: a guy with his tongue a little bit in his cheek, always conscious of the absurdities of his trade. The show was a hit and ran for five seasons, and Majors put his money where his mouth is and produced it himself. He brought back co-stars from past hits — Linda Evans from The Big Valley, Lindsay Wagner from The Bionic Woman, Richard Anderson from The Six Million Dollar Man — people with whom he had made something lasting, people he wanted to bring with him instead of leaving behind. It was the deed of a man who understood that loyalty was not a feeling but a way of life, a commitment to those who had journeyed with him.

The Fall Guy proved something very essential about Lee Majors. He wasn't some guy who used to be renowned, some flash in the pan from a certain era. He was a man with an instinctive sense of how to keep going in a famously fickle industry. It was quite an achievement: three different hit programs over three decades — The Big Valley in the sixties, The Six Million Dollar Man in the seventies, and The Fall Guy in the eighties. Such sustained, long-term success on American television is practically unprecedented, a credit to his versatility, his timelessness, and his professionalism.

Six years after his divorce from Farrah was finalized, Lee Majors married former Playboy Playmate, Karen Velez in 1988. They had three children together a daughter, Nikki and twin boys, Dane and Trey. The marriage lasted until 1994, when it ended in what their spokeswoman called a "amicable" divorce, a Hollywood term commonly used as a polite euphemism for a separation so traumatic that both parties have voluntarily agreed not to offer any details in public.

As he drifted into the peaceful years of his fifties, Lee Majors made a deliberate choice to retreat from the continuous glare of the spotlight. He began to choose smaller, more chosen jobs, and to live a more secluded life. He moved to Florida and began a ten-year retreat from the heart of Hollywood. This was not a time to hide. This was not a time to disappear. It was more a matter of deliberate repose, a conscious re-calibration after decades of active public activity. The two are fundamentally different.

Regular appearances on film in the 2000s were a welcome sight for legions of fans. He married Faith Cross in 2002. She was thirty years his junior and was a model, actor and writer. He was sixty two at the time. Inevitably the age difference drew its quota of attention and speculation. But the answer came in the marriage itself, which has now lasted more than twenty years, the longest and most settled of his four marriages. They decided to settle in Houston, Texas, deliberately distancing themselves from the never-ending machines of Hollywood. In 2003, he faced a significant health issue when he underwent heart surgery to clear a clogged artery, and he met and overcame it with the same matter-of-fact tenacity he had brought to every other obstacle life had placed in front of him. In 2019, when asked about his long marriage to Faith, he told People magazine with a quiet contentment that spoke it all, “It is a record in Hollywood.” It was the comment of a man who had finally gotten something truly right, and who had no need to make a huge production of it. He made a noteworthy cameo in the 2024 feature film version of The Fall Guy, a delicate nod to his own legacy, present without being needy, a stance he has maintained his whole public life.

In June 2009, in Farrah Fawcett’s final, excruciating weeks, Lee Majors picked up his phone. On her sixtieth birthday. In forty minutes they talked of a lifetime. They talked her life, the disease that was eating her up and the years they had spent together without giving. Lee Majors only does private acting when the most vital things are at stake, so close friends secretly and without fanfare reported it. A goodbye gathering of two kindred souls who had experienced so much together, a circle of compassion closing.

Farrah Fawcett passed away on June 25, 2009, at the age of 62. Her partner of 30 years, Ryan O’Neal, wrote in his 2012 memoir that her last words to him were: “I love you.” Lee Majors’ public statement was modest and respectful. “She fought a tremendous battle with a terrible disease,” he said. She was an angel on earth, and always. It was brief, polite and to the point, like a man who knew what to say and when to stop, a gracious and respectful adieu.

When her will was read, one of the things no one expected said everything without saying anything. Majors received $100,000 from Farrah Fawcett. Her 30-year companion, Ryan O’Neal, was by her bedside when she died and was the subject of her last recorded remarks. He got nothing. There is a complicated discourse behind just one detail and it contradicts every public narrative. No one involved has ever spoken publicly about that. Like most of Lee Majors' truest things, it's humble and whole. A tribute to an invisible friendship that withstood time, deception and public persona.

Lee Majors, 87, is still married to Faith and signs at fan conventions, where the fans are often as excited as in 1975. He speaks honestly about The Six Million Dollar Man, appreciating its ongoing importance in popular culture and what it meant to viewers.  He speaks about The Fall Guy with the quiet satisfaction of a man who understood he produced something meaningful, a project he was integral to from start to finish. He reveres Barbara Stanwyck, whose influence on his career was profound. He speaks of Farrah seldom and only after much deliberation, with restrained tenderness, as if he has concluded that decency and respect are more important than a thorough, public exposition of their difficult history.

He has never attacked Ryan O'Neal, used his name in a passion, or sat for an interview intending to portray himself as the victim of a decades-old public verdict. He kept working, showing up, and being the gentleman people always portrayed him as. There was no calculated warmth of a man sculpting a public image. This was true character—the kind that comes from recognizing the risk of losing something important and then deciding, with great strength, not to use it.

He carried a silent burden as an orphan at 18 months. His decision was made when old newspapers in an attic revealed his origins. He debuted in entertainment by competing against 400 men for a Western role. For five years, American television showed him in slow motion, making him a global icon. The woman in the poster he chose ran off with his buddy after he chose it. His final conversation lasted forty minutes before she stopped responding. She left him $100,000 in her will. Lee Majors has lived that narrative quietly for eighty-seven years. The woman he never forgot left a deathbed document proving she remembered him. Fundamental and enduring truths need not be uttered aloud to be fully and permanently understood.
