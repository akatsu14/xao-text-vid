A SALUTE OF STALE AIR

In the middle of the 1980s, the British monarchy was a study contrasted with. The institution behind the royal gates was inflexible, conservative and increasingly out of sync with a modernising Britain. The fairy-tale wedding of Prince Charles and Lady Diana Spencer had captured the world, but within the palace gates the institution was for the most part rigid. The average public wanted authenticity, a human touch against the cold impersonality of the spectacle. Then, out of the blue, comes Sarah Ferguson, a vibrant, red-haired daughter of a royal polo manager, Major Ron Ferguson, into this highly ordered society. It was built for a modest and virginal aristocrat. But she was not that sort of noblewoman. Later her own father would confess she was a woman with a past. She was a business woman, she had lived with a millionaire twenty years older than herself, she had an energy almost riotous, beyond control.

This seemed to be just the thing that aroused the curiosity of Prince Andrew, the sovereign's second son. Andrew was the first child of a reigning monarch since Queen Victoria and thus enjoyed a unique and privileged position. He'd been flying helicopters in the Falklands war, a fight that had briefly stirred a sense of nationalistic enthusiasm and made him the nation's pin-up prince. He was the swashbuckling, dashing war hero. The insatiable tabloid press christened him “Randy Andy” following a succession of sexual liaisons with beautiful ladies, including American actress Koo Stark. He was the charming spare to a more serious, older sibling, weighed down by duties. Royal family historians have observed how the Queen had a particular affection for Andrew, a maternal indulgence which may have led to a strong sense of entitlement and a belief that the usual standards did not apply to this particular person.

Ironically, it was Princess Diana who arranged the weddings. Diana, who was having a tough time succeeding under the restrictions of the royal system, saw in Fergie a kindred spirit and an ally. It was she who arranged for them to meet at Royal Ascot in 1985, an occasion which would become part of the history of York. It was a world apart from the traditional courtships of yore, the picture of Andrew gifting Sarah chocolate profiteroles in a joke. It was a wonderfully unanticipated, nicely normal experience. Here was a couple apparently in love with each other, who touched each other, and laughed. The crowd was ecstatic when they announced their engagement in 1986. Fergie was seen not as a pale imitation of Diana, but as an earthy, forceful contrast. While Diana was considered a delicate English rose, Fergie was described by one critic as being "like the wife of Bath". She was big and bold and unselfconscious.

In July of 1986, their wedding was a second taste of the royal romance that the people had been pining for and the nation cheered. "I walked in a commoner and I walked out a duchess." That was the thought that went through Sarah Ferguson's head as she walked down the aisle of Westminster Abbey. It was the ultimate Cinderella story. She leaned forward and kissed her new husband on the balcony of Buckingham Palace, a daring display of enthusiasm that cheered the throng below. This was a decision that defied the tight decorum that had governed previous royal appearances. But even in that time of success, there were a few who could see the warning signs. A veteran critic, observing their ecstatic laughter while filling out the registration form, is said to have whispered to a colleague, “That’s a marriage that’s not going to last.” The very qualities that made them so popular—exuberance and lack of decorum—were the roots that ultimately brought them down. They delivered a breath of fresh air, but one that would soon turn stale, and finally toxic, at some time.

THE GOLDEN CAGE AND THE LONELINESS OF THE ONE AND ONLY

The first few years of operation were gobbled up in a whirlwind of prosperity. The Duke and Duchess of York became the new power couple of the monarchy under them. Fergie defied expectations and became the first person to do so, as the first royal duchess to get a pilot’s licence. In 1987 they toured Canada and it was a triumph. The Yorks were more direct and active, compared to the more traditional and formal presentations of prior royals. They were funny, outgoing and relatable all in the same time. This new kind of royalty was one that interacted with people on a more human level than earlier kinds of royalty. There was a period when their brand shone, a perfect brand for the moment. Earlier, the Duchess of York, the Queen Mother, a woman of immense propriety and reserve. The change was astonishing and the public began to turn its opinion. But there was a more subtle story that began to take hold, that Fergie was a bad influence on Princess Diana. The famous pictures of the two sisters-in-law cracking up on a ski hill or stabbing an onlooker with their umbrellas at Ascot have been reinterpreted. What was once considered a joyful friendship is now depicted as Fergie leading a frail Princess of Wales astray, inciting an undignified frivolity that has riled Prince Charles and alarmed the royal old guard.

But underlying this public relations nightmare was a more serious and emotional crisis brewing. Diana, however, was praised for her slim elegance. It fed a deep insecurity, a frenzied hunger for affection and praise, a weakness that would be disastrous in the end.

TABLOIDS, TOES, AND TOTAL EXILE: THE PROBLEM IMPLODES

He was a loose cannon, like his ex-wife; but he was a loose cannon whose deeds were tacitly sealed by the British state.

What has been most intriguing about the story of the York family is the relation to one another through all their different calamities. They divorced in 1996 but they never really lived separately. They were still "best friends" according to Sarah. In 2008 she moved back to live with Andrew in his official residence, the Royal Lodge at Windsor. They vacation together, own a ski resort together in Switzerland, and are a family to their daughters. This has been a never-ending source of fascination for those who follow the royal family, many have depicted them as soulmates who, in a sense, are still a pair because of their emotional connection. Their own declarations of love for each other have been a constant supply of fuel for the rumour that they may maybe remarry at some point in the future.

But this codependent connection has also proved to be a destructive force for both sides. The Duke and Duchess of Disaster, locked in their own bubble, and no getting out of it. Together they rebelled against the world they had made their own.
