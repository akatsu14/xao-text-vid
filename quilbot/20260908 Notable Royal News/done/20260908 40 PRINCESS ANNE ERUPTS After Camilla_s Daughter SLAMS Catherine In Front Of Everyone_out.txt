THE WESTMINSTER CATCH

The scene was artfully calm, falsely so. A charity showcase for rural development was held at the Royal Horticultural Halls in Westminster, a great Victorian iron-and-glass construction. The air smelt of soil and wax polish. County produce sagged from trestle tables: jars of honey with handwritten labels, handmade cheeses, and at one specific booth, a proud display of Cumbrian seed potatoes. It was the perfect picture of benign, loyal monarchy, an institution quietly and uncontentiously going about its business of national life. Princess Anne, the Princess Royal, was working the room with the metronomic efficiency she has honed over five decades of public service. She was dressed in a smart navy coat, the only concession to glamour a gold brooch, surrounded by local dignitaries and producers. Her timetable was strict, her focus intense. By most public reckonings she does more official events than any other member of her family, a punishing schedule that is a testimony to a work ethic formed in the shadow of her mother, Queen Elizabeth II. In fifty years not a single official complaint against her conduct has ever been logged. She is, in every sense of the word, the load-bearing wall of the institution.

Catherine, the Princess of Wales, was two stands further along. Her appearance at a local agricultural showcase in June was no coincidence. Royal diaries are drawn out with military precision and palace sources later verified that her appearance had been arranged weeks in advance. The objective was apparent to produce a shot, a specific and striking image of continuity. The older woman, the family’s rock, who has never put a foot wrong in public, stood alongside the younger one, the future queen consort in whom the country has placed such tremendous trust. The monarchy has learned, under the long cold shadow of Diana, Princess of Wales, that its only reliable solution is continuity. The photograph of Anne and Catherine working in the same room was meant to be a modest statement that the House of Windsor had learned its lessons, that the future was secure and stable and in good hands. But someone outside the palace it seems, had also realised the significance of that photograph, and had resolved to break it apart.

The press pool was small that day, appropriate for the low-key affair. Two regional newspapers, one wire service photographer, and one television team filming B-roll without sound. And then there was the free lance. Located at the rear, on a long lens, with a diagonal line of sight across the hall. It was a thing that should have sparked instant questions. An outburst is something that happens out of nowhere, chaotic; the result of a chance encounter and a loss of control. An eruption doesn’t need a side door. No need for a silent name hand-added to a limited guest list. And it certainly doesn't need a camera already positioned on the one diagonal that can get both the accuser and the accused in one devastating frame. It was no fit of temper that had occurred in that hall. It looked in every respect like a delivery.

A CONSPIRACY OF CORRIDORS AND CAMERAS

To appreciate the significance of this statement one needs to understand what a royal estate audit is. It has nothing to do with personal spending habits or fancy hand bags. It’s a rigorous line-by-line reconciliation of what an estate spent vs what it was lawfully authorised to spend, frequently stretching back several years. Such an audit is meant to catch exactly two kinds of problem: money which went out with no paperwork behind it, and persons who were being paid for work for which no evidence can be presented. An audit alone does not accuse anyone. It only makes a list of things to be explained in some way. Someone in the wider royal family had reportedly been briefed to expect that list.

TWO ROYALS, ONE STRING

William was fighting his cold war from a basement in London and Princess Anne, in the back of her car, was doing her own systematic investigation. She didn’t use her cell. LATER AND HIDDEN COSTS

Fallout from the high-level controversy slapped Laura Lopez in the face. Queen Camilla, her mother, who has known how to manage any situation for 20 years, made no call. One harsher interpretation was that a thirty-year-old lady was keeping them invisible until the audit was done, the only thing that could rescue them.

The story goes beyond a two-minute argument. A major revolution in the British monarchy.
