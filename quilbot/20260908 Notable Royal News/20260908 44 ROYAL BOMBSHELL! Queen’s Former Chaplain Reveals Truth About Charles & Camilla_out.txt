THE CROWN IN CRISIS: QUEEN’S CHAPLAIN REVEALS CHARLES’S SECRET FAITH AND THE BETRAYAL OF A KINGDOM

A former chaplain to Queen Elizabeth II has broken decades of palace silence, alleging that King Charles III is actively undermining the very faith he swore an oath to defend. This is not a private spiritual journey; it is described as a deliberate act of cultural sabotage from within the walls of Buckingham Palace, with a weakened Church of England complicit in its own demise. Now, for the first time, we can reveal the Queen’s hidden fears for her son, the truth behind his constitutionally fraught marriage, and why his reign is being called the monarchy’s final, catastrophic act.

THE TROJAN HORSE IN BUCKINGHAM PALACE

In the hallowed halls of British power, the monarch is not merely a head of state; they are the Supreme Governor of the Church of England, the Defender of the Faith. This title, first bestowed upon Henry VIII by the Pope before his seismic break with Rome, is woven into the very fabric of the constitution. It is a sacred trust, a promise to uphold the nation’s established Protestant Christian identity. For seventy years, Queen Elizabeth II embodied that promise with an unambiguous, quiet devotion. But according to one of her own spiritual advisors, Dr. Gavin Ashenden, her son and heir is not her successor in faith, but its subverter.

Dr. Ashenden, a man who operated within the ecclesiastical circles of the Royal Household, paints a disturbing portrait of a King whose spiritual compass is fundamentally broken. He argues that King Charles is not a Christian in the traditional sense, but a disciple of the Swiss psychoanalyst Carl Jung. “I've always thought that Charles is a good Jungian more than he's a good Christian,” Ashenden revealed, explaining that this worldview flattens the profound distinctions between religions into a generic, therapeutic spirituality. For Jung, the specific god one believed in was irrelevant; the act of believing was what mattered for psychological well-being. For a constitutional monarch, this is not a harmless philosophical quirk; it is a dereliction of a sworn duty.

This Jungian lens, Ashenden claims, has rendered the King incapable of discerning the vast, irreconcilable differences between the figure of Jesus Christ and the Prophet Muhammad. “He he cannot tell the difference,” Ashenden states with chilling certainty. “He appears to be quite as enamored, enthralled, inspired by Muhammad, the prophet warlord, as he is by Jesus, the Messiah.” This is the core of the accusation: that the man anointed in Westminster Abbey, with his hand on the Bible, sees the central figure of his own faith as interchangeable with the founder of another.

The implications are staggering. The British monarchy, as it exists today, was forged in the fires of the 16th-century Reformation and solidified by the Glorious Revolution of 1688, which explicitly barred Catholics—and by extension, any non-Protestants—from the throne to secure the nation from foreign religious influence. The entire constitutional settlement is designed to prevent a monarch from having divided loyalties. Ashenden’s critique suggests that Charles has not just divided loyalties, but has effectively switched them. He becomes, in this analysis, a "Trojan horse" at the heart of the establishment. While the nation’s constitutional defenses are geared to repel the historical threat of Catholicism, a new and far more potent challenge in the form of Islam has entered through the front gate, welcomed by the King himself.

This is not mere speculation. Charles, as Prince of Wales, famously mused about changing his future title from "Defender of the Faith" to "Defender of Faith," a seemingly inclusive gesture that constitutional scholars immediately recognized as a potential constitutional earthquake. His public praise for Islam, his quoting of the Quran, and his patronage of Islamic art and philosophy have been well-documented. But viewed through Ashenden’s framework, these are not the actions of a multicultural monarch building bridges; they are the actions of a man who, as one commentator in the source material analogized, is like the CEO of McDonald’s publicly praising the vegetarian options at Burger King. It is a betrayal of the brand he is meant to embody, a catastrophic failure to defend his own product. He is, in the chaplain’s devastating assessment, “contributing in the destruction of Christian culture in his country.”

A CHURCH THAT FORGOT ITS GOD

If the King has abandoned his post, where is the institution he is sworn to lead? The Church of England, according to Dr. Ashenden, is no longer a bulwark of faith but a hollowed-out vessel, as spiritually adrift as the monarch it was meant to guide. “The problem is the Church of England is as woke or as Jungian or as un-Christian as Charles,” he asserts. This is not a church that can provide a moral check on a wayward King, because it has embraced the same secular, therapeutic ethos that has captured him.

For centuries, the Church of England, despite being an arm of the state, retained a measure of moral authority. The Archbishop of Canterbury, in particular, was a voice that could, and did, challenge power. It was the firm stance of Archbishop Cosmo Lang that was instrumental in forcing the abdication of Edward VIII in 1936, making it clear that the King could not marry a divorcée and remain on the throne. The Church held the line, defending the sanctity of marriage and, by extension, the moral integrity of the Crown.

That Church, Ashenden argues, is dead. In its place is an institution that has exchanged salvation for therapy, holiness for happiness. It has, in his words, “forgotten who it is and what to do.” The modern Church of England, in this view, is consumed by a desire to be seen as inclusive and non-judgmental, a goal that has led it to abandon its core theological principles. The concept of Hell, of divine judgment, of the unique truth of the Christian gospel, has been quietly shelved in favor of a more palatable, secular-friendly message of personal fulfillment. “They are concerned that people should be happy and be fulfilled,” Ashenden explains. “But this is a form of therapeutic Gnosticism. This has nothing to do with Christianity.”

This institutional decay created the vacuum that allowed for the constitutional crisis of Charles and Camilla’s marriage. When the heir to the throne, a divorced man, wished to marry a divorced woman, the Church of the 21st century lacked the moral conviction of its 1936 predecessor. Instead of a firm "no," there was a fudge. Instead of defending doctrine, there was a compromise. The internal debates were fierce, with a dwindling minority of clergy arguing that marrying divorced persons was against the teachings of Christ. But the tide had turned. The dominant culture within the Church was one of accommodation.

The ultimate decision—to deny a church wedding but permit a civil ceremony followed by a "blessing"—was a masterpiece of Anglican equivocation. It was an attempt to appease everyone that ultimately satisfied no one and, most critically, resolved nothing. It signaled that the rules still existed, but they no longer truly mattered. The blessing, held at St. George’s Chapel, Windsor, was a televised spectacle designed to look and feel exactly like a wedding, a deliberate act of liturgical misdirection to grant a veneer of religious legitimacy to a union the Church could not, at the time, formally sanctify. It was, Ashenden implies, a blessing of something the Church should have considered unholy, a public demonstration of its own moral and theological collapse. It was a church blessing failure, rather than absolving sin.

THE QUEEN'S HIDDEN ANGUISH

Behind the palace walls, as her son’s personal life and spiritual wanderings became a matter of public crisis, Queen Elizabeth II maintained her famous stoic silence. But that silence, Dr. Ashenden suggests, concealed a deep and profound maternal and monarchical anxiety. She was a woman of genuine, uncomplicated, and deeply held Christian faith. Insiders have long reported that her private life was structured around prayer and weekly worship, a constant in a life of tumultuous change. “The queen was unambiguously Christian,” Ashenden confirms. “She had a very real faith.”

This faith was not merely personal; it was the bedrock of her reign. It informed her sense of duty, her commitment to service, and her understanding of her role. She was, in the medieval concept, a monarch of "two bodies": the mortal woman, Elizabeth Windsor, and the immortal institution of the Queen. Ashenden describes her as a "Russian doll," with the private woman hidden deep inside the public role. Her genius was in never allowing the inner person to compromise the outer office. The world only caught glimpses of Elizabeth Windsor—the ecstatic horse owner at Royal Ascot, her face alight with pure joy as her horse won a race. For the rest of the time, she flawlessly played the role of Queen, a symbol of unity and stability above the fray of politics and personality.

It is through this lens that her fears for her children, and particularly for Charles, must be understood. “It would be very surprising if the Queen and Philip didn't look at their children and scratch their heads and say, 'What's going to happen when we're gone?'” Ashenden muses. They were watching heirs who seemed incapable of, or unwilling to, make the same sacrifice of self that she had. Prince Harry’s public emotional outpourings were the most obvious example of a royal who “can’t play a role at all,” letting the inner person erupt with disastrous consequences. But Charles, in a more subtle and arguably more dangerous way, was doing the same. His public advocacy for alternative spiritualities, his emotional candor in interviews, and his chaotic personal life were all instances of the man failing to subordinate himself to the office.

The Queen, for all her mastery, was not infallible. The crisis following the death of Diana, Princess of Wales, in 1997 was her gravest test. The country convulsed in a paroxysm of public grief, a "psychotic meltdown" as Ashenden terms it. The public, ignorant of royal protocol, saw the bare flagpole at Buckingham Palace not as a sign that the monarch was not in residence, but as a cold-hearted snub to the "People's Princess." The rule was clear: the Royal Standard is never flown at half-mast, as it signals the monarchy itself is diminished. But the public demanded a symbol of shared grief, and the Queen’s adherence to tradition was perceived as a lack of humanity. The monarchy itself teetered. It was only when an advisor reportedly warned her, “Your Majesty… if you want to be queen next Monday morning you need to lower that flag,” that she relented. It was a stark lesson: even for a monarch as revered as she, the ancient rules could be shattered by the force of public emotion.

One can only imagine her thoughts, then, as she watched her son navigate his own, self-inflicted public relations disasters. She had nearly lost the throne over a flagpole. He was risking it for an affair, for a muddled spiritual quest, and for a stubborn refusal to choose between his personal happiness and his constitutional destiny. Her anguish was likely twofold: the private pain of a mother watching her child struggle, and the public despair of a Queen seeing the thousand-year-old institution she had dedicated her life to preserving being jeopardized by the very person meant to ensure its survival.

A MARRIAGE OF CONVENIENCE, A CRISIS OF CONSTITUTION

The marriage of Charles and Camilla in 2005 was not simply a royal wedding; it was the culmination of a constitutional crisis that had been simmering for decades. It forced a direct confrontation between the modern desire for personal happiness and the ancient, rigid demands of the Crown. The solution was a compromise that bent the rules of church and state to their breaking point.

The historical precedent was clear and ominous: Edward VIII’s abdication. In 1936, the King’s desire to marry Wallis Simpson, a twice-divorced American, was an absolute barrier. The government and the Church were united: the King could not marry a divorcée whose ex-husbands were still living and remain monarch. He was forced to choose, and he chose the woman over the throne. For Charles, the situation was remarkably similar, yet the outcome was entirely different. He was a divorced man, and his intended, Camilla Parker Bowles, was not only divorced but had been married in a Catholic ceremony.

This Catholic dimension adds a layer of complexity often overlooked. According to the strict teachings of the Catholic Church, a sacramental marriage is indissoluble except by death. Without a formal annulment—a declaration that the marriage was never valid in the first place—Camilla was, in the eyes of the Vatican, still married to Andrew Parker Bowles. Ashenden confirms this stark reality: “Under Catholic Church, the Catholic Church sees that Camila is still married to Andrew Parker Bowles.” This means that the future King of England and Supreme Governor of the Church of England was, from the perspective of the world’s largest Christian denomination, marrying another man’s wife.

Faced with this constitutional minefield, the establishment opted for a legal workaround. The Royal Marriages Act stipulated the terms for royal unions, and while it had been updated, the core issue remained: the monarch’s marriage is a matter of state. The decision was made to proceed with a civil ceremony at the Windsor Guildhall, a secular solution to a religious problem. This in itself was a radical break with tradition. No senior royal in direct line to the throne had ever married in such a manner. It was a tacit admission that the Church of England could not or would not sanction the union.

The Queen’s role in this is a matter of intense debate. Sources suggest she could have drawn a hard line, just as the establishment had with her uncle. She could have told Charles: “Marry Camilla, by all means. But in doing so, you forfeit your right to be King.” This would not have been a crisis without a solution. The line of succession is long, and as the source material correctly notes, Parliament ultimately has the gift of the throne. They could have offered it to the next in line, Prince William, bypassing a generation and preserving the moral integrity of the Crown.

Why didn't she? The answer may lie in a mother’s love, a weariness with family conflict, or a pragmatic assessment that the monarchy in the 21st century could not survive another abdication crisis. Whatever the reason, the choice was made to accommodate Charles, and in doing so, a dangerous precedent was set. The rules, it turned out, were not absolute. The sacred duties of the monarch could be negotiated. The institution would bend to the will of the individual, not the other way around. It was a short-term solution that may have inflicted long-term, perhaps fatal, damage on the authority and mystique of the Crown.

THE KING WHO WOULD NOT BE KING

Long before he ascended the throne, there were profound questions about Charles’s character and fitness for the role. Dr. Ashenden’s critique goes beyond theology and touches upon the man himself, questioning his intelligence, his awareness, and most critically, his integrity. “Charles is not very clever,” he states bluntly, noting his poor academic record and a public speaking style that does not suggest a “developed brain.” While this may seem a harsh and patronizing assessment, it speaks to a perceived lack of the intellectual and spiritual wisdom required for the complexities of kingship.

The ultimate test of his character, in the eyes of his critics, came with the public exposure of his affair with Camilla. The infamous "Tampongate" scandal of 1993, when a secretly recorded, deeply intimate phone call was leaked to the press, was a moment of profound humiliation. Ashenden argues that this was the point of no return. The fantasy Charles expressed—of being reincarnated as Camilla’s tampon—was not just a private embarrassment; it was a public exposure that shattered the necessary mystique of a future king. “I don't think you can have your public persona contain that kind of exposure and then say, ‘I'm going to be king and be an example to you all,’” Ashenden contends.

This, he argues, was the moment for a man of integrity to step aside. The honorable path would have been to relinquish his claim to the throne in order to live his life with the woman he loved, free from the scrutiny and the hypocrisy. “He should have said, ‘I want to be happy with my wife. I'm going to marry her… I'll leave being king to somebody who doesn't face these dilemmas,’” Ashenden proposes. “That would have been a matter of integrity.”

That he chose not to suggests a powerful combination of selfishness and entitlement. It implies a man who believed he could have it all: the woman, the crown, and the public’s respect, regardless of the cost to the institution he was born to lead. This perceived narcissism was on display in his 1994 interview with Jonathan Dimbleby, where he not only admitted to his adultery but also appeared to mock the very origins of the Church of England he was destined to command.

This lack of awareness appears to extend to his duties as King. Ashenden points to two colossal failures of leadership. First, the state’s failure to protect hundreds of thousands of young white girls from rape and exploitation by grooming gangs, a national scandal that has received no meaningful response from the monarch whose duty is to protect his subjects. Second, the erosion of freedom of speech in the United Kingdom, where new laws threaten to criminalize personal opinion. “One of the most essential things a king would need to do is to provide freedom of speech,” Ashenden insists. On both counts, the King has been silent.

He is a monarch, Ashenden concludes, who is betraying not only his living subjects but the entire lineage of his ancestors. Every Christian king and queen for a thousand years who built and defended the culture he now presides over would, in this view, hold him accountable for an act of profound treachery.

THE UNRAVELLING OF A KINGDOM

The reign of King Charles III is unfolding against the backdrop of a nation in crisis. The United Kingdom is facing what the source describes as one of the worst religious, cultural, and financial crises in its history. And the King, far from being a point of stability, is seen as an active participant in the chaos. The Queen’s greatest fear—that her heir was unfit to rule—appears to be materializing in real time.

The consequences of his leadership, or lack thereof, are profound and far-reaching. By embracing a syncretic, multicultural spirituality, he has effectively disestablished the Church of England in principle, if not in law. He has signaled that the monarch’s faith is a personal preference rather than a constitutional pillar. This opens the door to a future where the separation of church and state becomes a political inevitability, severing a link that has defined the nation’s identity for 500 years.

His actions risk fracturing the very kingdom he reigns over. The moral clarity that Queen Elizabeth II provided is gone, replaced by an ambivalence that leaves people feeling rudderless. When she spoke, even in the darkest days of the pandemic, her simple assurance that “we will meet again” carried the weight of a lifetime of steadfast duty. People felt reassured. Charles, the adulterous king who champions a faith many of his subjects see as incompatible with their own values, can never command that same trust.

The future of the monarchy itself is now in question. The institution survives on a delicate balance of tradition, public goodwill, and the perceived integrity of the sovereign. By prioritizing his personal life over his public duty, by appearing to abandon the nation’s foundational faith, and by failing to speak for his most vulnerable subjects, King Charles has severely weakened all three of these pillars. He has provided ample ammunition for the republican movement and has created a crisis of legitimacy that will inevitably be inherited by his son, Prince William.

The final, chilling warning from Dr. Ashenden is that of a coming civil war—not necessarily of arms, but of culture. It is a battle for the soul of the West, a clash between a Christian culture of freedom and choice, and an Islamic culture he describes as totalitarian and compulsory. In this battle, the King of the United Kingdom has, in his view, chosen the other side. He is a king who is not defending his realm, but presiding over its dissolution. The great, tragic irony is that the Queen’s long and dutiful reign may be remembered only as the quiet, dignified prelude to the reign that finally brought the whole ancient and magnificent edifice crashing down.
