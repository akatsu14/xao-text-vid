A BOARDROOM DESIGNED FOR TWO PEOPLE

The board of directors is the basis upon which responsibility rests in meaningful generosity. The conscience of the institution is the first line of defense against mismanagement, mission drift and the ever-present temptation of self-interest. It is known as the institution’s conscience. An effective board is deliberately diverse, including independent expertise from the disciplines of finance, law and the specific area in which the charity is working. They're the serious, unbiased voices. They're the voices that are contractually obligated to critique the founders, to analyze the financial sheets and ask the hard questions. For them the most essential thing is not the famous names on the letterhead but the money the contributors donate and the charitable purpose to which it is to be put. This is not just a suggestion, this is the internationally established best practice, the gold standard of organizational governance for non-profits, and what separates legitimate foundations from private slush funds.

Archewell Foundation filings reveal that its operations were conducted in a manner that was in flagrant violation of this foundational tenet. The charity, which was in charge of millions of dollars of public donations, had just two people on its board of directors – Prince Harry and Meghan Markle. It’s not a board, it’s a proposal of marriage. Governance specialists would not only say that this setup is inadequate, but also term it as a deliberate snub of oversight by the corporation. In such a setup, there is no independent trustee, no dissenting vote, no one at the table with the authority and the distance to look the founders in the eye and say, "No." It's not a good notion. This spending raises a number of problems. This is counterproductive.

Psychological dynamics are highly dangerous given the nature of the arrangement. Each and every act, from the release of money to the sanctioning of trip expenditures, is interpreted via the lens of a personal relationship. When there is a disagreement at work, it can rapidly spill over into a quarrel in the marriage. A financial plan has a problem like a marriage has a problem. The core premise of an independent fiduciary obligation, the legal need to act only in the best interest of the foundation, is made almost meaningless. Who was it that thought it not worth it to spend more than double the receipts of a year? What other board person? Her own husband? This arrangement turns a public trust into a private fiefdom, sheltered from the scrutiny it was supposed to have. AN AUDIT THAT NEVER TOOK PLACE

A federal tax form is a sworn statement in the tangle of rules that control the financial sector. Every box you check , every figure you say , is a legally binding representation to the government of the United States . One of the most essential boxes checked on the Archewell Foundation’s Form 990, the annual reporting return for tax-exempt organizations, was the one certifying that its financial records were audited by an impartial third-party agency. One of the best markers of a company’s financial integrity is an independent audit. An objective and impartial accounting firm conducting a full and detailed audit of an organization's financial statements. It is intended to verify that the money came from where the foundation said it came from and that it actually went to where the foundation said it went. This document is usually supplied on request by any reputable charity and is the final word on whether the charity’s finances are being handled appropriately.

This checked box would prove to be the cornerstone of a conundrum that Archewell would have to solve for years to come. Well, in response to this public announcement, Charity Watch did just what you would expect them to do by their name: watch. On January 22, 2024, the monitoring organization sent a formal letter to the foundation politely asking for a copy of the independent audit that Archewell had sworn existed. The request was ignored. On December 6th, 2024, towards the conclusion of the year, they tried again. Again there is silence. The third letter arrived on the sixth of January, 2026. After two years and three formal requests to the most respected non-profit watchdog organization in the country, the request has not even been acknowledged.

This creates a logical dilemma with no easy way out of it. The unwillingness to produce the audit results . Here, it's a choice between two things and both are pretty damaging. First, there is a clean and complete and unbiased audit available but the foundation is refusing to publish it for some reason which is not explained. Logic does not apply to this case. If there was such a document, the smart and right thing to do would be to release it immediately. It would shut up the doubters and prove that the money is all there, and every dollar accounted for. If you don't give it, you'd be doing a big ol' piece of self-sabotage.

Or there is the possibility of no audit or not an adequate audit or audit conclusions that the foundation does not want to be made public. This is the latter alternative, which is much more likely. This raises a lot of serious ethical and legal questions. It is a troubling violation of the law, from the realm of poor governance to the territory of possible misrepresentation to a federal agency, to certify on a federal tax document that an audit has been performed when it has not been performed or when it cannot be produced. The Internal Revenue Service is not a forgiving body when it comes to these kinds of anomalies.

The lack of sound was a verdict in itself. Charity Watch was unable to conduct their due investigation, leading them to have to leave Archewell unrated.  They flatly declared that the organization “does not meet transparency benchmarks.” That’s the non-profit equivalent of a restaurant failing its health inspection. It is a statement in particular that the general public can not be assured to know what is going on behind the doors of the kitchen. That was a tremendous blow for a foundation that was built on the personal brand of its founders, a brand built on authenticity and truth-telling. The foundation was hurt by the unwillingness to release the one document that may prove the foundation's financial honesty. It seems like they had something in the books they were attempting to hide for all they were worth.

NEW RETREAT REBRANDED

When an institution is under intense scrutiny for its financial procedures and transparency, the normal response is to expand the scope of the books, increase the amount of oversight and enhance the level of accountability. The Archewell Foundation, on the other hand, took the opposite route. On 19 December 2025 the foundation announced a major reorganization, a decision which shocked governance experts. The Archewell Foundation would be renamed Archewell Philanthropies under the new branding. But the press release that accompanied the announcement was full of positive language about expansion and new prospects. The new structure will enable the couple and their children to “build upon their global philanthropic endeavors as a family,” the statement said.

The word “expand” was a brilliant bit of misdirection in the world of public relations. It was not expanding. It was gutting this area. The reality on the ground. During the same week the announcement was made, three employees were fired, which is more than half of the staff. The director of programs and operations was one of them and was the person responsible for implementing the foundation’s charitable activity. He was a professional, making $146,000 a year. The big event was that James Holt packed up and returned to the United Kingdom. He had been with the couple since the day they were married, and was the long-standing executive director. This was hardly the behavior of a corporation that was about to expand its operations. This was a retreat, clearly.

But the most important change was hidden in the little technical details of the new architecture. Archewell was pushing toward a mechanism known as “fiscal sponsorship.” This often means that the organization no longer exists as a fully independent, stand-alone foundation, but is now part of another, larger institution. This is a common thing in laymans terms. The main result, from the viewpoint of the general public, is a major decrease in openness, albeit there may be appropriate administrative reasons for this change. Generally, the requirements for direct public reporting are less for a project that is financially sponsored than for an independent 501(c)(3) foundation.

It was quite amazing how well-timed this motion was. Just as the public and media and regulators were demanding more clarity — on the $3 million shortfall, the missing audit, the state delinquency, the spiraling costs of the Invictus Games — the Sussexes rearranged their entire operation into a structure that would legally require them to reveal less. This was done to comply with the requirements of the law. It was a deliberate move, masked as good re-branding, to recede back into the shadows. It was chosen to take the story away from one of crisis and contraction and give it one of development of the family. They may establish a subtle rhetorical barrier by using the phrase “as a family,” while at the same time framing the new entity in the language of family. That is a private thing. Family is a private issue. The message is that family members ought not to be subjected to the intense and intrusive scrutiny of strangers. They didn't solve the transparency problem while the pressure was on; they restructured it out of existence. It was the coldest, the shrewdest move in the whole story.
