THE APPLICATION OF A FIASCO

It began, as it always does in such cases, with a soft word of simulated expectancy. On 28 May, the social media account for As Ever, the lifestyle company owned by the Duchess of Sussex, wrote three tantalising words: “Arriving tomorrow”. It was a signal flare for a brand that was created on its founder’s mystique. A new skincare line, wasn’t there? As a wellness supplement? A follow up on the jam that was sent to the influential and got a lot of attention? The next morning when the curtain was pulled back it was not a blockbuster product that was exhibited but that of a box of matches. Matches that come as a ‘free item’ when you buy a four-candle ‘signature scent’ set - and costs an astounding $256, or about £190. These matches are branded and are a limited edition and cannot be bought individually.

The first reaction was confusion. Consumer products are an enormous universe but I think the matchstick represents a unique example of simple, pure functionality. It is a commodity so omnipresent and inexpensive that you could find it in every supermarket and corner store on the earth for a measly cent. It is a thing purchased not from desire but need. It is a tried and effective marketing stratagem to introduce an unglamorous object into the domain of “luxury,” but it is a dangerous one. For this reason, you need to have a near-alchemical faith in the power of a brand to transform the mundane into the magical. Offering for sale a paperclip of sterling silver is a bold act of self-parody for a corporation like Tiffany & Co. It’s a testament to a century of earned notoriety. That decision from a brand just starting off like As Ever created an immediate and shocking cognitive contradiction. The juxtposition was immensely intriguing to the internet, a merciless arbiter of such apparent abnormalities. Despite her reputation for a more authentic life outside the golden cage of royalty, the Duchess of Sussex, it seemed, was hawking one of the most common products in the world while pretending to be rare luxury.

Well, except for the product itself, which was just the spark. The advertisement photograph used to publicise the fire was the spark that set off the outburst. The photo in question was not a photo taken in a hurry; it was a piece of official brand promotion that had been vetted, approved and distributed. It was the visual handshake between the company and the potential clients. It was a very beautiful picture. In the foreground is a brand new box of matches. On top lay a single matchstick, burnt out, the head blackened. Also, the “hero” product that was buried behind it, was an As Ever candle and it had its flame burning deep within a crater that it had made. The wax didn’t melt evenly on the surface. Instead, it had "tunneled." That is, it had burned a narrow, straight vertical shaft straight down the center of the glass, leaving behind thick walls of wasted, expensive wax adhering to the borders of the glass.

To an untrained eye it would have appeared to be a slightly odd photograph. For anyone even slightly acquainted with premium home items or, more specifically, the ongoing conversation around the As Ever brand, it was a visual catastrophe. There will be a marketing photo. This is done in the attempt to create sentiments of aspiration and desire by showing the thing at its most flawless state. This illustration had the opposite effect. And it did it in a way that was both stunning and obvious: it revealed the very fault that had become the most frequent and damaging criticism of the corporation. The tunneling candle was no longer an anecdotal complaint of a disgruntled customer. It was now the face of the brand, enshrined in the marketing the brand itself had produced. The match was burned and put in the picture in such a deliberate way that it felt less like a prop and more like a grim metaphor. It was a symbol of a flame that had been snuffed out, of potential that had been squandered, of a promise that had not been fulfilled. To the outsider, the decision-making process that resulted in the publication of this picture is utterly incomprehensible. There is a golden rule of product marketing, and that is that you should never endorse your own critique. Marketing gurus and brand strategists would say this image completely violates this criteria. Someone, or a committee of someones, looked at a photograph that screamed “product defect”, and decided that that was the best way to encourage people to spend $256 on the item. The internet didn’t just mock, but was incredulously surprised. The joke wrote itself, really. A witless brand symbolized by a candle that had no wick. We were no longer talking about luxury matches but a basic lack of judgment that lay at the very foundation of the business.

A POOR FUNDAMENTALS LEGACY

The catastrophic matchbox photo did not emerge in a vacuum. The sky was black with storm clouds of customer complaints and quality control concerns that had been gathering for weeks. Lightning struck. It was a lightning strike.  One cannot truly appreciate the effect of the image without first appreciating the conditions which made it so explosive. The burn pattern of the candle was the main point and the photo did a great job showing that element of the study. “Tunneling” is possibly the most unethical word in the lexicon of high-end candle production. It's the most clear and unequivocal evidence that the product was manufactured improperly.

This behavior can be related to a host of identifiable, specific characteristics, none of which is associated with high-quality craftsmanship. This can happen if the wick is too small for the diameter of the container, meaning it doesn’t produce enough heat to melt the wax to the edges of the container. There is also a potential that it is a sign of a badly made wax blend, which is often one that contains cheaper paraffin and not high-quality soy or beeswax, and does not distribute heat unevenly. Luxury candle makers such as Diptyque, Jo Malone and Cire Trudon spend a lot of effort on research and development to avoid this result. The reputation is based on the fact that they can provide a clean and even burn that completely consumes the product, hence justifying the premium price tag. A buyer who buys a single As Ever candle for sixty-four dollars doesn't anticipate a product that is equivalent to an alternative that can be obtained at a supermarket for twelve dollars. The expectation is for performance that deserves the sixty-four dollar price. This expectation was not being met, as the continuous reports of tunneling taken into account were indicating.

To make matters worse there was an even more disturbing situation, reports of candles being supplied with no wick to properly use them. One of these reports was published by a well known online reviewer with a large following, which immediately converted a simple customer service issue into a story that quickly traveled throughout the internet. Another, very common, claim was somewhat similar to the first, but its origin was more intricate, involving a third-party vendor. The outcry over “wickless” candles has coalesced to make a dramatic story of poor-quality manufacture, however it is important to examine each individual charge with the utmost care. These were not insignificant teething difficulties for a company that was putting itself in the luxury sector, but fundamental weaknesses in the trustworthiness of the brand being positioned.

The brand had a strict “no returns” policy which added one last layer of annoyance to the whole experience the customer experienced with the brand. This is not an unusual strategy for some products, but combined with a premium price it makes for a transaction with potentially high risks for the customer. In doing this it effectively delivers the message that "We are so confident in the perfection of our product that a return will not be necessary." The entire responsibility for risk is placed on the consumer. If a consumer receives a tunneling product or, worse, one without wick, the no-returns policy changes from an expression of trust into a trap. With no institutional remedies available, the only place for unsatisfied customers to take their grievances was to the public arena of the internet.  Their posts on the internet detailing the mismatch between the aspirational marketing of the business and their real experience became the dominant narrative. Thus building up a reservoir of bad feeling that was just waiting for a spark to ignite from within. The snapshot of the matchbox started a spark. It proved every complaint and every suspicion, and it did it not from some bogus internet account but from the brand itself.

The past of brands is a ghostly apparition.

Ever has been a case study in the monumental struggle of turning personal recognition into a viable and valued commercial brand, as has been the case throughout its history. The enterprise did not have its current name at some time. It was originally launched under the moniker American Riviera Orchard, conjuring up a certain sun-drenched Californian lifestyle. But the name was quickly attacked by the legal and administrative authorities. According to reports, the U.S. Trademark Office officials highlighted concerns about the phrase “American Riviera” being a widely recognized geographic name for the Santa Barbara/Montecito area, therefore preventing any exclusive trademarking. It has been noted that closeness to already existing registered brand names generated more complications. The core branding plan already wasn’t delivering… before a single product shipped.

But the switch to the shorter and more abstract As Ever not only solved the trademark problem, but implied a retreat from the prior, more concrete aim. One of the most important strategic initiatives of the new company was the creation of the now famous jam. The plan was to create a very small batch of a product — in this case, fifty jars of strawberry jam — and get it into the hands of a carefully picked list of celebrities, influencers and high-profile contacts, rather than the general public. It was a textbook example of 21st-century luxury marketing. This initiative aims to create a halo of exclusivity and attractiveness. The social media posts these aspirational people produce are meant to serve as endorsements that appear to be natural in order to create a wave of public interest and the perception of a ‘must-have’ item before it’s even available for purchase.

But right from the start, the execution was closely observed. In terms of media attention, the jam was less essential than the list of recipients, which created a meta-narrative about who was and wasn’t in the Sussexes’ inner circle. Also, the anticipated deluge of slick, passionate material on social media never appeared. This is a larger issue. Some of the recipients did post wonderful acknowledgments, but the majority of the fifty beneficiaries were conspicuously silent right up to the finish. The campaign was designed to create massive demand and cultural cachet, but instead it elicited a lukewarm response that seemed out of proportion to the high-profile nature of the beneficiaries. “Scam jam” was only internet commentary, not an accusation of fraud, but it spoke to the sentiment of a public that saw a vast disconnect between the brand’s strategic aims and how the brand was really received in the real world. This lacklustre conversion rate from gifted product to public promotion was where the derisive online nickname ‘scam jam’ originated. The episode was the first tangible evidence that the company’s underlying premise – that Meghan’s notoriety and powerful network would instantly translate into financial success – was perhaps a misjudged concept. It felt like the firm was running on the premise that its founder, who was close to the royal family, was the product, and other things such as jam or candles were merely merchandise. No matter who was selling the candle, the market seemed to be saying that for it to be worth 64 dollars the candle had to be of great quality.

The price of hypocrisy is:

Against the many critiques that have been made against the As Ever brand, there is no comment that has been more strong, more specific or more long-lasting than the one stated by Meghan Markle herself almost 10 years ago. She was the creator of a successful lifestyle website called The Tig in the year 2016, which was a considerable period of time before her life would get associated with the British nobility. At a conference in Atlanta where she was advocating the philosophy of her website, she made a definitive statement about the values her organization believes in. “There are no candles on my website that cost a hundred bucks,” she remarked, chuckling. “That’s so annoying.”

That one term, annoying, has been used in every single criticism of her existing business venture in the years that have followed. Of course a person has the right to develop. A company plan written in 2016 doesn’t have to be the same ten years later. The Meghan of The Tig was working in a different market and with a different public image than the Duchess of Sussex, who now runs a premium luxury firm. She was selling her brand as accessible and relevant. The journey from approachable to aspirational is something many prominent persons explore.

Nevertheless, the power of the quotation lies in the unusual choice of words she elected to use. But it is important to notice that when she said the candles costing $100 were "expensive," "unnecessary," or "overpriced," she did not make any economic judgments. But she did choose the adjective "obnoxious." That is a moral choice. The word implies a flaw in character. It appears the sale of an item like this is not simply a business decision but rather an indication of a disagreeable and narcissistic attitude. In this perspective, the prices of extreme luxury commodities are not a corporate strategy but a societal breach.

The juxtaposition is devastating when you place it next to the current pricing list for As Ever which includes a trademark scent package for $256 and individual candles for $64 each. And for that reason no more criticism is needed. This is a direct contradiction written by the author himself that can not be easily reconciled by any amount of marketing spin. It is not merely a charge of changing one’s mind; it is a charge of hypocrisy, that she now exemplifies the precise characteristic that she once publicly denounced as a defect in her character. For those who criticize the brand, this quote from 2016 is the Rosetta Stone of how the general population thinks about the company. It is a strong suggestion that the corporation in question is not merely a commercial enterprise, but an enterprise that was founded on a foundation that was formerly judged unethical by the founder of the enterprise. Now, every product launch, every high price point, and every “limited edition” matchbox are viewed through the lens of that one, damning sentence.

MEMORIES FOR SALE

One of the products sold by the brand is the “Number 519” candle, a further exploration of the fraught and often controversial overlap between royal history and private enterprise. It’s an obvious reference to the 19th of May, the date of the spectacular royal wedding between Meghan and Harry in St. George’s Chapel in Windsor Castle. The marketing for the scent describes it as “a scent that evokes that day” with notes of “sunshine”, “blue skies” and the “joy” inspired by the day. It's a way to bottle and market a memory, a really intimate experience that has been turned into a consumer product that costs sixty-four dollars."

The idea itself is not an uncommon one in the world of perfume, where the sense of smell is intricately linked with both memory and emotion. The trouble, however, is the particular story that goes the rounds in connection with the making of the wedding preparations. For years, many media accounts and royal comments have spun a tale about the smell that was within the old St. George’s Chapel. These stories remain that there was a disagreement as to whether or not to insert more scent or atomizers into the chapel that was erected in the 14th century to perfume the air before guests arrived. There was said to be a lot of hostility to the plan from authorities within the Palace. They felt that the historic mood was meaningful and profound in the old chapel, which was a holy place and the burial site of 10 British monarchs. The tale has continued to circulate, and it was heightened when the luxury fragrance business Diptyque revealed that their products were used as part of the day’s preparations. This provided the story with a verifiable data point.

For loyal supporters of the monarchy, the story itself has become part of the established lore surrounding the wedding, even though the particulars of the episode continue to be contested. So when the bride from that wedding releases a candle that is obviously marketed as capturing the “atmosphere” of that morning, the candle is not launched into a neutral market. It sets off this already controversial story. Critics see the “519” candle not as a romantic tribute but a vulgar attempt to monetize a time already associated with a theme of battle over artificial vs true environment. This is the perception that critics have about the candle. Some individuals feel that it is a straightforward commercialization of a royal event, that it tries to sell a luxury product by using a title and a wedding that cost the British public. There are some disturbing questions about the boundary between private memories and public property, and whether or not a moment of such national and historical significance should be incorporated into a scent concept sold in a commercial catalogue.

This brought into question the brand's public assertions that they consistently have "sellouts." A question regarding the fundamental logic of business is brought up as a result of these findings. If there is a significant gap between the amount of demand for your products and the amount of supply, then there is little purpose in trying to encourage customers to make purchases. The practice of including "complimentary" matches in a candle bundle that costs $256 is one of the most common examples of gift-with-purchase strategies. These strategies are utilized to increase the perceived value of the product or service to the client, as well as to urge a customer who is hesitant to make a purchase. Whenever the objective is to encourage a purchase, they are most effective. The two signals, namely the assertions of an overwhelming demand and the utilization of promotional incentives, are in direct opposition to one another and constitute a perfect contradiction.

Additionally, Meghan herself has defined a deeper basic paradox that lies at the core of the brand, and this tension is a manifestation of that paradox. Within the context of an interview with Harper's Bazaar, she made a compelling argument regarding the value of maintaining one's own integrity. This strategy, on the other hand, is not compatible with the very idea of developing a lifestyle brand. The construction of an external judgment is typically and invariably the foundation upon which a lifestyle brand is built. The well crafted brand story, the carefully chosen imagery, and the aspirational pricing points are all instances of the tools that are deployed to influence and direct the perception of the audience. When it comes to a lifestyle brand, the opinions of other people do not pose a threat. It is essential to the operation of the firm. The success of the company as a whole will be determined by whether or not the general public believes that the product is worth the price that it bears and the promise that it makes.
