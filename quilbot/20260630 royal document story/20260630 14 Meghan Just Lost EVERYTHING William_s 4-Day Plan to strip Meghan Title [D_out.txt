The boundaries of authority are the Iron Cage of Parliament.

While the Royal Prerogative is known for its speed and ferocity, it is not without its limitations. Despite the fact that Prince William's power on the first day of his reign is enormous, it is not absolute. Within the framework of the monarchy, which is governed by a complicated constitutional structure, the King has the ability to act independently in certain domains, but in other domains he is constrained by the will of Parliament. A point that the majority of coverage of this drama utterly overlooks is the fact that this results in a two-speed system of royal power. Prerogative power is characterized by its speed, decisiveness, and the fact that it only requires a signature. The Letters Patent, which was responsible for stripping Prince Andrew of his styles in just four days, controlled the use of the title "Prince" and the term "Royal Highness." With this, William has the ability to take the HRH from Harry, Meghan, and their children relatively immediately, and it is quite probable that he will do so.  

But the removal of a peerage itself – the actual Dukedom of Sussex – is a far different and separate matter. In the United Kingdom, a peerage is not just a fashion statement; rather, it is an aristocratic title that is embedded in the legislative and constitutional structure of the country. The fact that a peerage can only be abolished by an Act of Parliament is clearly documented in the constitutional archives. It is not something that the King can just sign away. The implication of this is that even after William takes action, Harry would still be legally recognised as the Duke of Sussex, a prince who does not hold the title of Royal Highness. He would become a ghost within the machine, his basic title remaining the same but his royal honorific removed.

Another equally important position, that of Counsellor of State, is also subject to the same legislative obstacle. Under the Regency Act of 1937, a select group of senior royals have been granted the legal authority to act on behalf of the monarch in the event that he is absent from the country or unable to do so. Prince Harry is currently included on that list because of the position he has in the line of succession after the current monarch. Taking him out of the palace is not a straightforward decision. It has been made abundantly apparent by constitutional scholars at University College London that "Any further steps would certainly require legislation." Parliament does not move in a span of four days. A law that would modify the Regency Act would be subject to discussion, political negotiation, and examination from the general public. As a result, the current administration would be compelled to take a stance in a dispute between members of the royal family, which is a situation that both the Crown and Downing Street are working hard to avoid. This is the iron cage that restricts the power that the next King should have. It is possible for him to win the first battle in a matter of days; however, the war to completely remove Harry from his constitutional obligations would take several months or even years, and it may possibly come at a substantial political cost.
