In antiseptic, professional language, the group made the judgment in a formal statement that the foundation’s board of directors, consisting of just two members, “falls well short of widely accepted nonprofit governance best practices.” That phrase masks a much harsher reality. On the governance front for non-profits, a married pair as a two-person board is not just a red flag, it’s a bonfire. That is a lesson that has been learned many times in high-profile crises. Independent boards were not created as a bureaucratic formality. THE AUDIT THAT NEVER HAPPENED 

There are few papers of more importance to the world of financial accountability than an independent audit. The gold standard of transparency is a certification by an independent, third-party accounting firm that the books of an organization are clean and its financial statements are accurate. It is the verification that is believed to be without parallel. When it comes to the management of money, it is the ultimate display of respect to contributors and regulators. The Archewell Foundation checked the box on its federal tax form, the 990, a legal document that requires a signature under penalty of perjury, indicating that it had in fact undergone such an independent audit of its finances. Not a pledge, not a hope, but a vow to the facts as they were.”

With this public announcement, Charity Watch did what its mission calls for and requested to see the audit. The first formal written request was sent to the recipient on January 22, 2024. No one answered. December 6, 2024: Second letter sent. All silent again. A third and last request was made on January 6, 2026, bringing the cycle of inquiry to a stop after two years. The result, however, was the same: an audit was never generated.

This continued failure to reveal a document which the foundation has legally verified to exist for two years provides only two logical possibilities. The first is that there is a perfect and complete audit that sits in a filing cabinet in Montecito and that Archewell's leadership just decided to deny three straight requests from the top charity watchdog in the United States for reasons they have yet to explain. Logic is no good here. A clean audit would not be able to mask an institution that is reeling from a notice of delinquency, a diminishing donor base and public worries about its financial management. If it was produced with that in mind it would be the single most effective way to restore credibility and silence critics. Not only would it be absolutely free of charge, but the act of making it available would immediately serve as a demonstration of a dedication to transparency.

The second, much more likely scenario is that the audit, however it was certified on the tax filing, does not exist or contains conclusions so troubling that the leadership of the organization decided that stonewalling was a less destructive choice than disclosure. It is the conclusion that any regulator or skilled investigator would no doubt arrive at given the data, and it is the conclusion to which the established pattern of behavior leads. The lack of the audit is not a trivial administrative slip-up; it is a grave breach of confidence that has happened to the organization. In such a situation the concerns of financial management are turned from a question of guesswork into a question of provable non-compliance.

Charity Watch ultimately came to its own conclusion. The watchdog said that Archewell “does not meet transparency benchmarks” since it failed to obtain the most basic paperwork required for a legitimate assessment. That meant the watchdog couldn’t give Archewell a rating. A corporation that certifies in a legal file that it has the existence of a vital accountability document and then refuses to give the document for a period of two years is not experiencing a gap in transparency, for example. The organization is now pursuing a policy of openness, a policy of deliberate obfuscation to keep its internal financial operations from the scrutiny of the public and regulatory agencies. The commission of this conduct alone would be sufficient to generate severe concern for the office of the state Attorney General. On the other hand, it was only one piece of a much larger and more frustrating puzzle.
