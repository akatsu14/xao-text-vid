It speaks only when the constitutional integrity of the monarchy is in question, and its pronouncements are intended to be put into the permanent record of the state, unchallengeable in any court of law.

Ministries of government, the Metropolitan Police, municipal bodies across the United Kingdom, had been stuck in a condition of paralysing ambiguity for years. A WALL OF STATE AUTHORITY

To comprehend the catastrophic effect of the phrase “private citizens” one must delve past the hurt pride into the huge financial and legal machinery that supports the British monarchy. This was never about damaged feelings, or fancy names. It was power purchased by public money and the legal ability to call on armed protection under British constitutional law. Prince Harry had been fighting a tough battle in the High Court with the British government for three long years and had now lost decisively.

If a royal is a full time working member of the family their security is not a private affair. A strong, clandestine government group called RAVEC, or the Royal and VIP Executive Committee, runs it. It is a group of top officials from the Home Office, the Metropolitan Police and the Royal Household which assesses threat levels and sends elite, state-trained firearms police. You cannot hire a team of publicly trained police officers as a private individual the way you can hire a private limousine. That would create a two-tiered system of justice and security and privatise a core role of the state. If you are not acting as an official representative of the British state, you are not entitled to instruct its armed agents to clear London traffic for your personal convoy.

This is the brick block his entire campaign ran into. The Lord Chamberlain’s circular made it impossible in law for any government entity to justify the use of state resources. With one administrative ruling it had wiped out the whole basis of his claim. This put the spotlight firmly on his children, Archie and Lilibet, and a technique Prince William had seen coming a mile off. The Sussex camp regarded this as a win, an affirmation of their children’s royal position. But they gravely misread a basic element of the modern monarchy: a title is a badge of blood, not a passport to the public purse. A royal title does not automatically entitle you to state protection, taxpayer-funded residences or diplomatic courtesies. And Prince William, watching from Windsor, knew exactly what the Sussex strategy was. THE SPIRITS OF YORK

The Sussex closure was not drawn out as a response to Meghan Markle. It was born in 2011, a product of the harsh family infighting over Prince Andrew and his daughters, Princess Beatrice and Princess Eugenie. The palace receipts show that the Sussexes were not uniquely targeted; they were just the latest to fall foul of a ruthless standard of accountability that was set long ago, and to understand the present you have to exhume the past.

Today most people see Prince Andrew through the disastrous lens of the Epstein scandal that ended his public life. But the battle over his finances and the status of his household had begun a decade before. His daughters Beatrice and Eugenie were born legitimate blood princesses, styled Her Royal Highness from birth. But they were not, and never intended to be, working royals. They had private careers, worked for private firms and created private lives. The trouble was that they were living their private lives with the full security apparatus of senior working royals. They were accompanied by teams of armed Metropolitan Police bodyguards on ski holidays, yacht parties in the Mediterranean and exotic breaks. This private security for two non-working royals cost the British taxpayer a stunning £500,000 a year.

There was public outrage. In the grim aftermath of the 2008 financial crash, with austerity biting and people losing their jobs and homes, the notion of paying for police officers to carry Glock pistols outside luxury nightclubs in St. Tropez was politically radioactive.  Then Charles stepped in. He understood how dangerous this feeling of entitlement was for the long-term survival of the monarchy. He approached his mother, Queen Elizabeth II, and made an executive request to cut the Yorks’ security detail.

Prince Andrew strained all his strength. He sent furious, pleading letters to the Queen’s private secretary, he demanded palace interventions, he argued passionately that his daughters were being put at risk simply because they were the grandchildren of the sovereign. He took it as a personal affront, a diminution to belittle his branch of the family. But Charles, champion of a "slimmed-down monarchy" for three decades, held the line with unbending resolve.  He knew that an institution that protected private luxury over public responsibility was doomed in the 21st century. The security was formally lifted. Beatrice and Eugenie would henceforth have to pay for their own protection, like any other private citizen.

When Harry and Meghan eventually tried to depict their own security removal as a uniquely cruel and even racially motivated incident, the palace had the perfect retort. The Sussex argument, so appealing to American television audiences unfamiliar with this history, simply came up before the weight of this proven reality. 

A WAR FOR THE CROWN'S JEWELS 

The debate, however, was never just about bodyguards.  It was about something considerably more symbolic and guarded: the tangible contents of the royal vaults.  Specifically, it was about a single, beautiful masterwork of jewelry valued at over 10 million pounds—the Greville Emerald Kokoshnik Tiara. 

To realise the significance of this artefact, you must understand its holy status within the royal collection.  Crafted in 1919 by the famed Parisian firm of Boucheron, it was made for Dame Margaret Greville, one of the wealthiest and most important socialites in British history.  A stunning lattice of platinum and rose-cut diamonds, its design is dominated by a single, astonishing cushion-cut emerald weighing 93.7 carats.  It is a piece of almost legendary beauty and force.  When Dame Margaret died in 1942 she left her whole world-class collection of jewels to an institution, not a person. She handed it on to Queen Elizabeth, the Queen Mother, to keep for the nation in perpetuity under royal care.

Jump to the weeks before the royal wedding in May 2018. Royal brides are often summoned to Buckingham Palace to choose a tiara for the wedding day and Meghan Markle was no exception. Several royal insiders have said her eyes immediately alighted on the Greville emerald tiara. She desired that. She wanted it. And when the late Queen Elizabeth II politely but firmly informed her that this particular tiara was unavailable, the tension that had been simmering within Kensington Palace exploded into the infamous incident that staff still refer to as "Tiara-gate." It was in this context that Prince Harry reportedly shouted his now-infamous phrase, "What Meghan wants, Meghan gets." 

The Queen’s answer was as quick as it was cold. She is said to have told Harry to his face: “Meghan can’t have whatever she wants.” “She gets whatever tiara I give her.” Meghan ended up walking down the aisle with the stunning but less flashy Queen Mary’s diamond bandeau. But behind the scenes her appeal had lit three big fires in the organization. First of all, the Greville emeralds are in the care of the Royal Collection Trust. These are not the monarch’s personal trinkets, held in sacred trust for the British nation. They are historical objects, not trinkets to be stuffed into suitcases and flown to another continent. Secondly, palace advisers were increasingly worried about commercial exploitation. They had a profound fear that priceless, historic royal jewels loaned to someone who was simultaneously building a private, commercial lifestyle brand in America could end up being used as props on commercial streaming specials or magazine covers, diluting their priceless historical and cultural value.

Third—and probably most importantly—Prince William took it personally. He had taken a solemn oath to conserve not only the treasure of the Crown but also the private collection of his mother, Diana, for his wife Catherine and his daughter, Princess Charlotte. It was anathema to him that these precious pieces of history could be regarded as negotiable commodities in a transatlantic lifestyle endeavour. The fight over the tiara was the first indication that the Sussexes didn’t really understand the distinction between being a celebrity and being a member of a constitutional monarchy.

The message was obvious, these treasures are borrowed, never owned. They are a symbol of responsibility, not a prize of state.
