THE PAPER SAFETY NET

“There is no reasonable plaintiff who would enter that battle unprotected in a High Court trial of this nature that goes on for eleven weeks. The seven assertions were no different. They'd taken out what is known in legal circles as "After the Event" insurance, or ATE for short. This is a specialist policy bought after a court struggle has already started. Its only purpose is to cover the costs incurred by the other party if they lose. It is the financial safety net, the ultimate backstop, in the high-wire performance of English litigation. All seven claimants had insurance worth a combined sixteen and a half million pounds.

This is where the firewall was. " For Prince Harry this was meant to be the barrier between him and a financial calamity. That had been constructed to take the shock of a bad ruling. They went further and admitted that the shortfall could be 'to the tune of many millions of pounds'.

Not a single leak or rumor has been here. It is a formal submission to the court, in their own words. Those who had hold of the safety net took their own measurements of the hole in it, and then they told the judge how big the hole was. To grasp the importance of this topic, it is vital to be aware about the difference between a standard expenses order and an indemnity costs order. The default position, a standard order, allows the winner to recover costs that are "reasonable and proportionate". Any doubt in the assessment is to the favor of the losing party and bills are often cut by a considerable amount in such circumstances. It is vital to observe the punitive setting of the indemnity foundation. It is the legal system’s tool of last resort, for those situations, in which the legal system has tried everything else, when a party has engaged in litigation beyond the usual, aggressive back and forth of a court battle—behavior that might be characterized as irrational or in bad faith.

The concept of proportionality is rendered irrelevant when considering an indemnity order. This reverses the burden of proof and makes any doubt work for the winner instead of the loser. All of the bills have been paid almost completely. An insurance policy for £16.2 million presents a severe hurdle to a monthly expenses order. It's a picket fence versus an indemnification order on a cost of more than thirty-four million pounds. And, too, the persons who bought that fence have written to the judge saying they do not want for it to be upheld. Now the whole story of Harry's financial destiny hangs on one legal term, and not anything else. The question that has to be answered at this stage is not how big the bill is but will the judge go for the punitive setting or not. In the weeks after his loss, Prince Harry gave the other side just the argument they needed to turn that eventuality into a terrifying possibility.
