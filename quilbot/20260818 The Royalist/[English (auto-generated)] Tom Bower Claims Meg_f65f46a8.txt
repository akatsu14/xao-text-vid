THE MAKING OF A ROYAL BIRTH

To realise the seismic nature of Bower's claims, it is essential to understand the tremendous, almost religious, significance of a royal birth in the British political system. For ages the birth of an heir has been far more than a family matter: it is the core of dynastic stability, the guarantee of a peaceful transfer of power. The monarch’s very legitimacy hinges on an uninterrupted, traceable succession line. This is not only tradition, but a survival strategy created in the embers of civil war and succession crisis.

The most notable example is the Warming Pan controversy of 1688. When the Catholic wife of King James II, Mary of Modena, gave birth to a son, James Francis Edward Stuart, rumours quickly began to circulate that the real heir had been stillborn and that another baby had been smuggled into the birth chamber in a warming pan. This conspiracy notion, driven by anti-Catholic feeling, helped spark the “Glorious Revolution” that overthrew James II and set William and Mary on the throne. The incident burnt a lesson into the monarchy’s institutional memory: the circumstances of a royal birth must be beyond all doubt.

The principle of having a senior minister of the Crown, usually the Home Secretary, present at the birth of any possible heir to the throne, was retained for the next 250 years. It was no ceremonial nicety. It was a constitutional requirement. The minister was there as an independent witness on behalf of the state, to see for himself that the 'warming pan' deceit had not been employed and that the kid presented to the country was, in fact, the child born to the royal mother. This procedure, which seems to modern sensibilities outdated and intrusive, was stopped only shortly before the birth of Prince Charles in 1948.

In its stead a new system of verification came into being, less intrusive but no less demanding. The Queen’s own doctors, a team of the country’s most distinguished medical specialists, would oversee the delivery. No one doubted what they said. The modern state’s assurance of legitimacy was their signatures on a birth notice displayed on a ceremonial easel outside Buckingham Palace for the world to see. They were the witnesses who had no axe to grind. That is what makes the claims about the birth of Archie so terribly disturbing for constitutional traditionalists. Bower’s inquiry found that Meghan had refused to involve the Queen’s trusted doctors. Instead she chose her own private medical staff. This was a break with a strong, symbolic tradition. The judgement took away, from the institution's point of view, the same layer of independent verification that was supposed to stop the whispers and conspiracy theories that have tormented the monarchy for ages. It produced a vacuum of certainty and it is into that vacuum that all the later uncertainties have poured.

To the casual reader, this may look like a meaningless semantic quirk, a modern or Americanized turn of phrase. But in the carefully regimented language of royal communication, each word is loaded with meaning. Legal and linguistic scholars have pointed out that "born to" indicates a direct, biological and physical tie. In contrast, "born for" is more unclear. It can also mean purpose or identification, not physical origin. This may imply nothing, but when considering all the other unsolved questions—the missing documents, the rejected physicians, the contradictory timeline—this strange choice of words was just another small piece of a much larger, and extremely baffling, puzzle to investigators. THE GHOSTS OF THE NURSERY

The dispute lies well beyond public curiosity and deep into the arcane but essential field of constitutional law. The British monarchy is not a private family but a constitutional institution controlled by ancient laws and modern statutes. The line of succession is not a recommendation, it is a legal fact. And at the heart of that reality lies a concept that has not altered for a millennium: an heir must be "born of the body."

The phrase is explicit in texts like the Act of Settlement 1701. This indicates that a person in the line of succession must be the direct, biological and physically delivered offspring of their antecedents. Adoption or surrogacy, however admirable and acceptable ways for private persons to become parents, have never been part of the direct line of succession. This is why the core question as posed by Bower's sources is so powerful. The argument is not just whether the children have Harry’s DNA. A DNA test would simply confirm paternity. The far more constitutionally significant subject, the arguments say, is that of motherhood. Who had the children?

That’s a major difference. If for any reason the children are not biologically born of Meghan’s body, their place in the line of succession – currently sixth and seventh – might become the subject of formal, legal challenge. According to insiders close to Bower’s palace, this is not just a theoretical problem being debated on online forums. "There's a quiet, serious look by constitutional lawyers at the implications," he says. It’s not necessarily an endorsement of the Sussexes’ narrative, but it does represent the sheer gravity of the issue. To admit the situation would bring on a constitutional crisis immediately. To ignore it leaves a potential time bomb ticking at the heart of the institution.

The long-term repercussions for the monarchy are huge. For King Charles III, whose reign has been dedicated to the notion of a slimmed-down, stable and modern monarchy, a legitimacy problem involving his own descendants is a nightmare scenario. For Prince William, the Prince of Wales, it is a future threat to the integrity of the kingdom he may one day inherit. If the legitimacy of the sixth and seventh in line is shrouded in significant doubt, it undermines the whole organization. It leaves an open sore to be preyed upon by anti-monarchists, and will forever put a question mark over the family.<br/><br/> The stability of the British throne depends upon the public’s unqualified faith in the legality of the succession. But reports after the Sussexes' private ceremony in California stated enquiries made to the Church of England and the Archbishop of Canterbury discovered no equivalent entry or official acknowledgement in their archives. Privacy appears to be a shield to be drawn only when difficult questions are asked. The clarity that was not provided has created a vacuum, and in that vacuum the most harmful inferences are reached. Perhaps, as Bower’s work reveals, the true issue is far wider and more complicated than a simple DNA finding. Perhaps the controversy is not about whose DNA the infants bear, but about the fundamental, verifiable circumstances of their coming into the world.

Until the papers are produced, until the medical records are unsealed, until the story can be placed beyond all reasonable dispute, the controversy will remain exactly where Tom Bower's investigation left it: unfinished, unresolved, and forever waiting for that one, single piece of evidence powerful enough to either vindicate a prince and his wife, or to shake the oldest monarchy in the world to its very foundations.
