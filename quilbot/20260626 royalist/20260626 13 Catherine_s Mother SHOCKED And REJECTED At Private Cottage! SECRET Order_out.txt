GHOST OF DIANNA

To understand the origins of this covert battle, one must comprehend the deep psychological traumas that plague the House of Windsor, and in particular the woman who now sat on the throne beside King Charles. Camilla’s path to the throne was one of the longest and most difficult in contemporary royal history. For decades, she was “the other woman,” a pariah, the villain of the mythical narrative of Charles and Diana. She faced years of unremitting vilification by press and public, a trial by fire that would give anyone a keen sense of the fragility of the monarchy and the corrosiveness of public opinion. She had the crown but she never really had the hearts of the people. Then came Catherine.

For the palace old guard, and for Camilla herself, it turned out, this tremendous popularity was not a benefit. It was a threat. So her plan could never be a head-on clash. A public feud would be a disaster, putting Camilla once again in the role of villain, and Catherine in the role of victim, a perfect repeat of the past. quiet exile and a new order

Revolutions are seldom declared in the murky world of the British monarchy. Power doesn't change with a press release, power changes with a subtle but evident shift in the institutional milieu. It was no longer a safe career decision to follow her lead.

Meanwhile, the steady stream of bad press about the Middleton family in pro-royal media sites dwindled to a trickle, and finally ceased. The change in editorial policy was neither explained to the public nor did it need to be. The message had gone home. Camilla herself was seen less and less at public engagements. Within weeks it was seen that she was spending the most of her time at Ray Mill House, her private property in Wiltshire, out of the hub of power in London. No notification was made concerning any change in her role or status. She was the Queen still. But she had been quietly expelled from the heart of the decision-making apparatus of the monarchy. She had lost her leverage.

For Catherine the transition was rapid and dramatic. Soon afterwards Carole Middleton was back at Adelaide Cottage. This time the gates opened without hesitation. The public may not have known the details of the internal struggle, but they could perceive the change. In public appearances Catherine exuded a new confidence. She looked less guarded, more self-assured. It was as if a big weight had been lifted. The media portrayal of her family became warm and joyous.

What this whole affair proved was that Catherine, the “commoner” from Berkshire, had been woefully undervalued by the very institution she married into. The old guard perceived her origins as a liability to be managed. They did not know that was the source of her greatest power. She did not cower, nor did she retreat in the face of a years-long campaign of psychological attrition. She looked into it. She collected evidence. She forged alliances. But she met her adversary face to face, not with hereditary privilege, but with reality. In the process she not only won a personal struggle but may have spared the monarchy from its own darkest, self-destructive inclinations. Catherine’s refusal to sacrifice her family on the altar of a faulty and archaic notion of stability revealed that the future of the Crown is not in the purity of its bloodlines but in the character of those who wear it. She fought back. She won. And in doing so, she redefined what it meant to be Queen.
