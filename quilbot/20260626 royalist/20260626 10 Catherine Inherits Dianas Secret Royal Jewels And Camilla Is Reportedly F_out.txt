As for the Diamond and the Dagger

The first sound to be heard waking up on the morning of May 12th was the peaceful and almost reverent hum of climate-controlled air within the Jewel House at the Tower of London. Here, beyond the walls of the ancient fortress, are the Crown Jewels, the physical embodiment of the British monarchy. This collection of precious stones is more than a collection; it is the sum of a thousand years of power, conquest and divine right converted into gold and diamond. A senior technician from Garrard, the famous royal jeweler, arrived, it seems, in this holy place with a set of instructions that, even if they were high-profile, looked run-of-the-mill enough. The task was to delicately extract the Cullinan III and IV diamonds from their setting. Within the Royal Family group these diamonds are affectionately known as ‘Granny’s Chips’. Those acquainted with the palace said the ultimate aim was to remake and modernize the outfit so it might be worn by Queen Camilla at a future state banquet.

At first look it appeared the same. The Crown Jewels are not museum pieces frozen in time but living artifacts of history, often reshaped, reset and reimagined by successive generations of queens and consorts. Queen Mary was a famous master in the art of altering jewellery and is known to have had jewels reset in her lifetime. Many of these modifications were specially commissioned by Queen Elizabeth II to suit contemporary tastes and needs for ceremonial use. The desire to enhance the setting of two of the world’s most famous diamonds was significant, but many would have read this as a prerogative of the Queen Consort, as it would have been an opportunity for her to put her own personal stamp on the general aesthetic of the monarchy. But this time it was different. Now a line had been crossed, a hidden tripwire triggered.

Before the jeweler's tools could even approach the historic setting, the entire procedure was brought to a stunning and instantaneous halt. Security protocols were emphasized, usually considered a formality. The vault’s atmosphere was calm and professional, but it was immediately interrupted within moments by the emergence of an authority that was substantially more powerful than the ordinary. The claims involve a delegate linked to the Privy Council, a historic council of advisors to the Sovereign, accessing the vault. They were not bringing a polite request for delay but a legal injunction, a piece of paper that immediately stopped the process. There was a directive to the Garrard technician to get closer. The jewels, however, were not to be touched. In minutes a small modification to a piece of jewellery had snowballed into a constitutional and legal issue, in the most secure and private room of the British state.

The shock waves that moved through the royal rings were profound and immediate. It was almost unthinkable, legally, that a directive, no doubt given by the King for his Queen, should be thwarted. It was a direct affront to the general perception of the power the monarchy had. But the reasons for the injunction were even more startling, exposing a strategic bet laid out years in advance by a monarch whose intelligence continues to inform the Crown from beyond the grave. It was not a curatorial objection or mistake in running the organization that led to the closure. It was the very product of Queen Elizabeth II, building a legal fortress, one purposefully built and very well built.

They were no ordinary diamonds whatever. This collection comprised things that were deeply tied to the public image and personal history of the late Queen, pieces that marked her reign and the very essence of the modern monarchy. She effectively removed them from the conventional line of inheritance and control, by placing them in a private trust, and placing them under a new and far more strict set of regulations than they would otherwise be under.

Such a deed requires an understanding of the complex financial state of the monarchy. The Crown Jewels are not the property of the king. They are held in trust for the people. The Sovereign, on the other hand, has a huge private collection accumulated over many generations via inheritance and gift. The private collection is the one that can be left by will in accordance with the unique intentions of the monarch. Queen Elizabeth II, a woman known around the world for her fastidious attention to detail and fierce loyalty to the institution she led for seventy years, appears to have used this distinction to pull off a move that was breathtakingly brilliant in its strategy. She was attempting to create a system by establishing a legal organization outside the direct day-to-day authority of the palace that would run automatically and according to its own strict logic.

It was argued that the provision, the essential component of this trust, which had now been brought into play, was simple and devastating. The trust was created for the sole purpose of protecting the assets. It was a legislative time capsule meant to ensure these specific emblems of royalty would be kept for future generations in their original form and unchanged. The preservation clause was broken the moment the order was given to take the Cullinan III and IV diamonds apart. And the results were predictable . The trust agreement had a transfer provision frequently called a dead man’s switch. This clause would be invoked if the main purpose of the trust was breached.

The impact was immediate and brutal in its aftermath. It was relocated direct to Kensington Palace, the residence and power base of the Prince and Princess of Wales and taken out of the jurisdiction of Clarence House, the official residence and office of the King and Queen. Catherine was the mother of the future King George and at a terrifying moment became the only authority and legal guardian over some of Queen Elizabeth II’s most cherished treasures. It seemed like the late Queen had reached from the past to make sure that the legacy of the monarchy would not go to her son’s wife but to her grandson or granddaughter.

Eighteen minutes later, the coup d’etat

There was a palpable apprehension in Clarence House. Aides said there was a desperate scramble to contain the issue and undo the legal decree. At first it was assumed this was a mistake, because it was a bureaucratic mess a word from the King could straighten out. Allegations say they made steps to have the vault reopened before noon to carry out the task as arranged and eliminate any risk of an issue. On the other hand, they were met with a wall of legal steel. According to sources, royal attorneys, who’s main job is to save the Crown from constitutional issues, immediately refused the plan. Their advice was that the trust was a private legal organization. While it existed, it did not lie immediately under the King's jurisdiction. That it would be a constitutional nightmare for King Charles to use his royal prerogative to overthrow a private trust founded by his own mother would be a constitutional nightmare. This would be a story that would make catastrophic headlines and raise questions about the rule of law, as it would be seen as a monarch using the power of the state to interfere in a private legal battle when it was in fact a private matter. ‘It is in no way acceptable that the King, who is the embodiment of British law, should break it for his own personal ends.
