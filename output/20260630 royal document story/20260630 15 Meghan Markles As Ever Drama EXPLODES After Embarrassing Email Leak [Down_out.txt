THE MATCHSTICK THAT SET AN EMPIRE ON FIRE: INSIDE THE CATASTROPHIC LAUNCH THAT EXPOSED THE TRUTH ABOUT MEGHAN'S BRAND

A single photograph was supposed to launch a new luxury product. Instead, it may have burned an entire brand to the ground. The image, approved by Meghan Markle's team, featured a box of matches and a candle so poorly made it became an instant symbol of catastrophic failure. We are about to reveal why this wasn't just a mistake, but a confession—a visual admission of the deep, systemic fractures running through a post-royal empire struggling to justify its own existence.

**THE ANATOMY OF A FIASCO**

It began, as these things often do, with a whisper of manufactured anticipation. On May 28th, the social media account for As Ever, the Duchess of Sussex’s lifestyle brand, posted three tantalizing words: "Arriving tomorrow." For a brand built on the mystique of its founder, this was a signal flare. Was it a new skincare line? A wellness supplement? A follow-up to the much-discussed, influencer-gifted jam? The next morning, the curtain was pulled back to reveal not a blockbuster product, but a box of matches. Branded, limited edition matches, presented not for individual sale, but as a "complimentary item" bundled inside a four-candle "signature scent" collection, retailing for a formidable $256, or approximately £190.

The immediate reaction was one of bewilderment. In the vast universe of consumer goods, the matchstick occupies a unique space of pure, unadorned utility. It is a product of necessity, not desire; a commodity so ubiquitous and inexpensive that it is available in every corner store and supermarket on the planet for mere cents. The act of elevating this humble object into the realm of "luxury" is a classic, if perilous, marketing gambit. It requires an almost alchemical confidence in a brand's power to transform the mundane into the magnificent. For a brand like Tiffany & Co., selling a sterling silver paperclip is an act of audacious self-parody, a testament to a century of established prestige. For a nascent brand like As Ever, it was a move that created an immediate and jarring cognitive dissonance. The internet, a ruthless arbiter of such perceived absurdities, found the contrast irresistible. The Duchess of Sussex, a figure who had famously sought a more authentic life away from the gilded cage of monarchy, was now seemingly peddling one of the world's most common items under a veneer of exclusive luxury.

But the product itself was merely the kindling. The inferno was ignited by the promotional photograph chosen to announce it. This was not a hastily snapped picture; it was a curated, approved, and published piece of official brand communication, the visual handshake between the company and its potential customers. The image was stark. In the foreground, the new box of matches. Resting atop it, a single, spent matchstick, its head blackened and charred. And behind it, the "hero" product: an As Ever candle, its flame burning deep inside a crater of its own making. The wax had not melted evenly across the surface. Instead, it had "tunneled," burning a narrow, vertical shaft straight down the center, leaving thick, untouched walls of expensive, wasted wax clinging to the sides of the glass.

To a casual observer, it might have been a slightly odd photograph. To anyone with even a passing familiarity with luxury home goods, or indeed with the ongoing conversation surrounding the As Ever brand, it was a visual catastrophe. A promotional photograph is a promise. It is meant to depict the product in its most ideal state, to create aspiration and desire. This image did the precise opposite. It showcased, with alarming clarity, the very defect that had become the brand's most persistent and damaging criticism. The tunneling candle was no longer just an anecdotal complaint from a disgruntled customer; it was now the official face of the brand, immortalized in its own marketing. The burned match, placed so deliberately in the frame, felt less like a prop and more like a grim metaphor—a symbol of a flame extinguished, of potential wasted, of a promise unfulfilled. The decision-making process that led to this image's publication is, to outside observers, utterly baffling. Marketing experts and brand strategists would argue that such an image violates the most fundamental rule of product promotion: never validate your own criticism. Someone, or a committee of someones, looked at a photograph that screamed "product defect" and decided it was the perfect way to entice customers to spend $256. The internet erupted, not just with mockery, but with a kind of stunned disbelief. The joke wrote itself: a witless brand, represented by a wickless candle. The conversation was no longer about luxury matches; it was about a fundamental lack of judgment at the very heart of the operation.

**A HISTORY OF FLAWED FOUNDATIONS**

The disastrous matchbox photograph did not land in a vacuum. It was a lightning strike in a sky already dark with storm clouds of customer complaints and quality control concerns that had been gathering for weeks. To understand the full impact of the image, one must first understand the context that made it so explosive. The central issue, the one that the photograph so perfectly encapsulated, was the candle's burn pattern. In the world of high-end candle manufacturing, "tunneling" is the cardinal sin. It is the most visible and undeniable indicator of a poorly constructed product.

The phenomenon has specific, identifiable causes, none of which are associated with premium craftsmanship. It can occur when the wick is too small for the diameter of the container, unable to generate enough heat to melt the wax all the way to the edges. It can also be a sign of a poorly formulated wax blend—often one using cheaper paraffin instead of high-quality soy or beeswax—that doesn't distribute heat evenly. Luxury candle houses like Diptyque, Jo Malone, or Cire Trudon invest immense resources in research and development precisely to avoid this outcome. Their reputation rests on delivering a clean, even burn that consumes the entire product, providing value for the premium price tag. When a customer pays $64 for a single As Ever candle, the expectation is not for a product comparable to a $12 supermarket alternative; the expectation is for performance that justifies the sixty-four-dollar price. The persistent reports of tunneling suggested that this expectation was not being met.

The problem was compounded by an even more alarming issue: reports of candles arriving without any usable wick at all. One such report came from a prominent online reviewer with a significant following, instantly amplifying a single customer service issue into a viral news story. A second, similar claim also circulated, though its provenance was more complex, involving a third-party seller. While treating each claim with due diligence is important, the combined noise of "wickless" candles created a powerful narrative of shoddy production. For a brand positioning itself in the luxury space, these were not minor teething problems; they were foundational cracks in its credibility.

Adding a final, frustrating layer to the customer experience was the brand's stringent "no returns" policy. This is not an uncommon policy for certain goods, but when paired with a luxury price point, it creates a high-stakes transaction for the consumer. It effectively says, "We are so confident in the perfection of our product that a return will not be necessary." It places the full burden of risk on the buyer. When a customer then receives a product that tunnels, or worse, has no wick, the no-returns policy transforms from a statement of confidence into a trap. With no official recourse, frustrated customers had only one place to take their grievances: the public square of the internet. Their online posts, detailing the gap between the brand's aspirational marketing and their lived experience, became the dominant narrative, creating a reservoir of negative sentiment just waiting for a spark. The matchbox photograph was that spark. It confirmed every suspicion and validated every complaint, not from an anonymous online account, but from the brand itself.

**THE GHOST OF BRANDS PAST**

The trajectory of As Ever has been a case study in the immense difficulty of converting personal celebrity into a viable, respected commercial brand. The venture did not begin with its current name. It was first unveiled as American Riviera Orchard, a name that evoked a specific, sun-drenched Californian lifestyle. However, the name immediately ran into bureaucratic and legal headwinds. Reports indicated that the U.S. Trademark Office raised concerns, as the term "American Riviera" is a well-known geographical designation for the Santa Barbara and Montecito area, creating obstacles for exclusive trademarking. Further complications reportedly arose from similarities to existing registered brand names. Before a single product had been shipped, the foundational branding strategy had faltered.

The pivot to the shorter, more abstract "As Ever" solved the trademark problem but signaled a retreat from the initial, highly specific vision. The new brand's first major strategic move was the now-infamous jam launch. The methodology was textbook 21st-century luxury marketing: produce a highly limited run of a product—in this case, 50 jars of strawberry jam—and distribute them not to the public, but to a meticulously curated list of celebrities, influencers, and high-profile friends. The goal is to create a halo of exclusivity and desire. The social media posts from these aspirational figures are meant to act as organic-seeming endorsements, generating a wave of public interest and the perception of a "must-have" item before it's even available for purchase.

The execution, however, was scrutinized from the start. The media coverage became less about the jam itself and more about the list of recipients—a meta-narrative about who was in, and who was out, of the Sussexes' inner circle. More critically, the anticipated deluge of glossy, enthusiastic social media content never truly materialized. While a handful of recipients did post polite acknowledgments, many of the 50 remained conspicuously silent. The campaign, designed to project overwhelming demand and cultural cachet, instead produced a lukewarm response that felt disproportionate to the high-profile nature of the recipients. This underwhelming conversion rate—from gifted product to public promotion—gave rise to the derisive online nickname "scam jam." While the term was merely internet commentary and not a factual claim of fraud, it accurately captured the mood of a public that sensed a significant gap between the brand's strategic ambitions and its actual reception in the world. The episode provided the first hard evidence that the core assumption of the business—that Meghan's celebrity and powerful network would automatically translate into commercial success—was perhaps a flawed premise. The brand appeared to be operating as if its royal-adjacent founder was the product, and items like jam or candles were merely merchandise. What the market seemed to be indicating was that for $64, the candle itself had to be exceptional, regardless of who was selling it.

**THE PRICE OF HYPOCRISY**

Of all the criticisms leveled against the As Ever brand, none has been more potent, more specific, or more enduring than a single quote, uttered by Meghan Markle herself nearly a decade ago. In 2016, long before her life would become intertwined with the British monarchy, she was the proprietor of a successful lifestyle blog, The Tig. Speaking at a conference in Atlanta to promote her site's ethos, she made a clear statement about her brand's values. "There are no $100 candles on my site," she said, followed by a laugh. "That's so... obnoxious."

In the years since, that one word—obnoxious—has echoed through every critique of her current business venture. A person is, of course, entitled to evolve. A business strategy from 2016 does not need to remain static a decade later. The Meghan of The Tig, positioning her brand as accessible and relatable, was operating in a different market and with a different public profile than the Duchess of Sussex, who now heads a premium luxury brand. The repositioning from accessible to aspirational is a standard journey for many public figures.

However, the power of the quote lies in the specificity of her word choice. She did not say that $100 candles were "expensive," "unnecessary," or "overpriced." Those are economic judgments. Instead, she chose "obnoxious." This is a moral judgment. The word implies a character flaw. It suggests that the act of selling such an item is not merely a business decision, but a reflection of an unattractive, pretentious personality. It frames extreme luxury pricing not as an economic strategy, but as a social transgression.

When that quote is placed alongside the current As Ever price list—a $256 signature scent bundle, individual candles at $64 a piece—the juxtaposition is devastating. It requires no additional commentary. It presents a direct, self-authored contradiction that no amount of marketing spin can easily reconcile. The charge is not simply one of changing one's mind; it is a charge of hypocrisy, of now embodying the very quality she once publicly condemned as a character flaw. For critics, this 2016 quote is the Rosetta Stone for understanding the public's reaction to the brand. It suggests that the venture is not just a commercial enterprise, but an enterprise built on a foundation that its own founder once declared to be morally distasteful. Every product launch, every high price point, every "limited edition" matchbox is now viewed through the lens of that single, damning word.

**MEMORIES FOR SALE**

The brand's product line contains another item that delves into the complex and often fraught intersection of royal history and private commerce: the "Number 519" candle. The name is a direct reference to May 19th, the date of Meghan and Harry's spectacular royal wedding at St. George's Chapel in Windsor. The marketing copy describes the scent as an evocation of that day, promising notes of "sunshine," "blue skies," and the "joy" of the occasion. It is, in essence, an attempt to bottle and sell a memory—a deeply personal moment transformed into a $64 consumer product.

The concept itself is not unusual in the world of fragrance, where scent is intrinsically linked to memory and emotion. The complication, however, lies in the specific and widely circulated backstory associated with the wedding's preparations. For years, multiple media reports and royal accounts have detailed a story concerning the scent inside the historic St. George's Chapel. According to these persistent narratives, a discussion arose about introducing additional fragrance or atomizers into the 14th-century chapel to scent the air before guests arrived. The suggestion was reportedly met with some resistance from Palace staff, who felt that the ancient chapel, a sacred space and the final resting place of ten British monarchs, possessed its own profound and sufficient historic atmosphere. The story has remained in circulation, later bolstered when the luxury fragrance brand Diptyque indicated their products were used as part of the day's preparations, lending a verifiable data point to the narrative.

While the precise details of the episode remain disputed, the story itself has become part of the established lore surrounding the wedding for close followers of the monarchy. Therefore, when the bride from that wedding launches a candle explicitly marketed as capturing the "atmosphere" of that morning, it doesn't launch into a neutral market. It activates this pre-existing, controversial narrative. For critics, the "519" candle is seen not as a romantic tribute, but as a brazen attempt to monetize a moment that is already associated with a story of conflict over manufactured versus authentic atmosphere. It feels, to some, like a direct commercialization of a royal event, leveraging a title and a wedding paid for by the British public to sell a luxury good. It raises uncomfortable questions about where the line is drawn between personal memory and public property, and whether a moment of such national and historical significance should become a fragrance concept in a commercial catalogue.

**THE CRUMBLING FACADE**

The series of missteps from As Ever—the underwhelming jam launch, the quality complaints, the self-sabotaging photograph—must be viewed against the backdrop of the Sussexes' broader financial and media strategy. Their landmark 2020 deal with Netflix was initially reported as a sweeping, multi-year partnership, a cornerstone of their new, financially independent life. Over time, however, the nature of that relationship appeared to shift. Reports described a change to a "first-look" deal, a structure that industry insiders recognize as a more limited and less financially secure commitment. More recently, reports emerged that Netflix had exited its involvement with the consumer products side of the Sussexes' business altogether.

While unconfirmed by either party, this narrative was given credence by other observable data points. There were accounts of unsold products, like teas and baking mixes, being distributed internally after sales allegedly fell short of expectations. A website glitch was also reported to have briefly exposed inventory data, raising questions about the brand's public claims of consistent "sellouts." The specific question these observations generate is one of fundamental business logic. If demand for your products genuinely and dramatically outstrips supply, why would you need to incentivize purchases? The inclusion of "complimentary" matches in a $256 candle bundle is a classic gift-with-purchase strategy. Such strategies are designed to add perceived value and encourage a hesitant customer to complete a transaction. They are most effective when a purchase needs encouraging. The two signals—claims of overwhelming demand and the use of promotional incentives—sit in direct contradiction to one another.

This tension points to a deeper conflict at the heart of the brand, one articulated by Meghan herself. In an interview with Harper's Bazaar, she stated, "The moment that you start making all of your personal decisions based on external judgment, then you lose your authenticity." It's a powerful statement about personal integrity. Yet, it is fundamentally at odds with the very nature of building a lifestyle brand. A lifestyle brand is built almost exclusively on the cultivation of external judgment. The curated images, the aspirational price points, the carefully crafted brand story—all are tools designed to manipulate and guide the perception of an audience. External judgment is not a threat to a lifestyle brand; it is its lifeblood. The success or failure of the entire enterprise hinges on whether the public judges the product to be worthy of its price and its promise.

The matchbox launch did not create these contradictions. It did not invent the quality control issues or the 2016 quote about "obnoxious" candles. What it did, with shocking efficiency, was gather all these disparate threads of criticism, doubt, and hypocrisy and weave them into a single, unforgettable image. A burned-out match. A tunneled, failing candle. It was a photograph that spoke a thousand words, and every single one of them was a confirmation of the public's growing suspicion that behind the glossy facade of As Ever, there is an operation that is fundamentally struggling with its purpose, its quality, and its own identity. It was the moment the brand's carefully constructed narrative finally went up in smoke.
