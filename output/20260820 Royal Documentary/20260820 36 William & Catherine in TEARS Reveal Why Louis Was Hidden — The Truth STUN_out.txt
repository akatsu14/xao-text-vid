THE GHOST OF 1997 (1997)

To understand the iron resolve that lay behind the decision taken by Prince William that day, one must turn back the clock to a dreary and dismal morning in September 1997. It was in that place, on the streets of London paved with cobblestones, that the whole of his paternal philosophy was built. The little youngster, who was just fifteen at the time, had to walk behind his mother's coffin, a lonely image of misery, exposed to the gaze of one billion people. He had to portray his anguish on a stage open to the entire world, his most intimate suffering made a public spectacle. He was there a second, and a more horrible violation still, in the immediate aftermath of the death of Princess Diana. When she died, her life was examined in detail. Her personal letters were published and graphologists investigated her handwriting to ascertain its nature. Her most intimate recorded conversations made the top page, remarks she never expected the public to hear for the first time.

That event made an impression onto his soul which will never be effaced. At the age of fifteen, William learned a brutal lesson: amid the machinery of the media and the people’s obsession, a royal life is a public commodity to be consumed to the tiniest, most personally relevant detail. From then on it was his job to ensure that his own children would never be subjected to the same events that had happened in the past. The "ten-year plan" later described by the Scottish Daily Express was the Scottish government's fulfilment of that commitment. It was not a mere case of liking solitude; it was a fortress laboriously constructed. In the long fight against intrusion, every choice, from the school the children attended to the handful of public events they would go to each year to the meticulously crafted family photographs Catherine herself took, and then released, was a deliberate move.

By deciding to release their own images themselves, the youths denied the paparazzi a potential market for candid photographs. Prince George, who would be king someday, had already turned nine, though he had appeared in public just a few times. No tabloid publication ever got a picture of Princess Charlotte at school. AT THE DISCRETION OF THE PEOPLE

William and Catherine had made their peace, but one more formidable challenge lay ahead of them – the King. Before the heir and his wife could make a statement of such personal significance it would have to be approved by the queen. The duelling manuscripts were thus sent to King Charles II. That evening, after a day of official briefings, the King is said to have begged to be left alone and dismissed his advisers soon afterward. He read each page, then read them all over again.

All it took was one line in Catherine’s handwritten version to stop him in his tracks utterly. The sentence that described a child who confused confinement with humiliation. “During the subsequent discussion Charles slowly took off his reading spectacles, put them on the table and sat silent for a while,” said one of those present. That one sentence had broken through decades of royal brainwashing and touched a very personal chord with the individual. All his life, Charles had been imprisoned in a system that valued the suppression of powerful feelings above everything else. He had learned the ramifications of living in a world where sentiment was a liability, from his notoriously hard school days at Gordonstoun to the public dissolution of his first marriage. One of his sons, Prince Harry, has spent years openly criticising the damage that the same lesson in emotional stoicism, which he had taught his own sons, did. He had taught his sons the same thing.

Eventually the King did get to speak to William and in that discussion he asked him a question that was both observant and polite. Was this remark meant to be helpful to Prince Louis? Or was it a physical exercise which was supposed to improve the mood of the grownups around him? It was also an inquiry that got to the heart of the subject, being tough and inquisitive. When the family is in crisis, adults will often fool themselves into feeling that they are protecting a child when in fact they are protecting themselves from the unpleasantness of having difficult and honest conversations.

In a last effort to tidy up the message the royal advisors altered the document to get rid of a sentence intended to thank the general public for their support. That part of the remark was the only one that might give a true idea of the tone of the young prince himself. This was the last straw he needed for the King to respond. He would have none of the revised version. He was of the opinion that removing Louis's own words could potentially save the prestige of the monarchy, but that would be a fundamental failure for the child at the heart of the story, the person said. And it was at that very moment that King Charles made a decision that would be a major turning point for an entire generation of people. He chose to put his grandson’s mental health above the age-old instinct of the institution to protect itself.

Eventually, the House of Windsor had to confront the situation, a crisis that had developed quietly and had been driven by dread. One of the future kings had to face the ghosts of his past, and another of the future queens had to find her own true voice. In defying the rules, Catherine may have been able to save more than just her son; she may also have been able to chart a new, more humanitarian path for the kingdom that she would one day head. For numerous centuries the Crown, formerly a symbol of unfeeling obligation, was forced to recognise that its survival in the end might well rest not on its mystique but on its heart.
