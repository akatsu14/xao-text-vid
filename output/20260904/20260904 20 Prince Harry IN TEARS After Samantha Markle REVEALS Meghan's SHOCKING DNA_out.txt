The mystery of 1997 is not just a matter of speculation; it is anchored to a specific, verifiable event that has fueled years of suspicion: the sealing of public records. Long before Samantha Markle’s claims of DNA evidence, online researchers digging through California’s public databases reported finding a birth entry for that year under the name “Rachel Meghan Markle.” On its own, such an entry could be a clerical error or a case of mistaken identity. But what transformed this digital curiosity into a cornerstone of conspiracy was what happened next. The timing of this action is the single most inflammatory detail in the entire saga. December 2018 was a critical juncture. Meghan had married Prince Harry in a globally televised ceremony just seven months earlier. She was pregnant with their first child, Archie, and was a full-time working member of the Royal Family. To critics, the sequence of events screamed of a cover-up, a reactive move to bury something inconvenient just as Meghan was solidifying her place within the monarchy.

Understanding the legal context is crucial. Records in the United States can be sealed for numerous legitimate reasons, including the protection of minors, the finalization of adoptions, or to correct significant administrative errors. Public figures often take legal steps to protect their privacy from an intrusive press. However, the act of sealing a record, particularly one of such potential significance for a new member of the British Royal Family, is a double-edged sword. While it restricts access, the act itself creates a permanent marker of suspicion. It signals that there is something there worth hiding. Legal analysts suggest that for a figure like the Duchess of Sussex, such a move is strategically risky. It invites precisely the kind of forensic investigation it is meant to prevent.

Legal experts find this puzzling. A false claim of such a serious medical procedure would typically be considered a clear-cut case of defamation, easily disproven with medical documentation during a legal discovery phase. The absence of such a definitive public or legal rebuttal has allowed the rumor to fester.

Now, place this unresolved medical question alongside Samantha Markle’s new claim: that she possesses DNA evidence linking Meghan to a child born in 1997. If Samantha’s evidence is real, it would prove that Meghan was, at one point, physically capable of childbirth. This would not automatically disprove the hysterectomy rumor—such a procedure could have happened later—but it would add another explosive layer of complexity to her history. But it could also compel Meghan to produce a vast array of personal documents: her own DNA for comparison, medical records, private communications, and any documentation related to the 1997 sealed records.
