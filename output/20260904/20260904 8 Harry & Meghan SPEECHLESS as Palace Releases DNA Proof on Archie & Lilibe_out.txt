For centuries, one steady assumption has underpinned the British monarchy. It is the integrity of the bloodline. It is a doctrine which has survived wars, revolutions and crises of abdication; it is a rigid code where legitimacy is not a question of opinion but biological fact. According to the long-standing and unbending requirements of the Crown, an heir must be born of the body of a titled royal mother inside a legitimate marriage to be eligible for inheritance. It is a criterion so rigorous that it appears almost mediaeval in the society of today, but it is one that is vital to the whole fabric of hereditary control. If this were to be contested then it would bring into question the very existence of the monarchy.

The Letters Patent granted by King George V in 1917 stipulated that the grandchildren of the monarch in power at that time were to be titled as His or Her Royal Highness and to bear the titles of Prince and Princess. Under the legislation, Archie and Lilibet, as grandchildren of the newly crowned King Charles III, were suddenly declared eligible for these titles.

The goal is to create a more modern institution, more efficient with its resources, less sprawling. In this context perhaps the King would not see much justification to grant full royal titles to grandchildren who are living as private citizens in another country and who will never be called upon to conduct public obligations.

If the King wanted to formally prevent Archie and Lilibet from using their official titles, he would have to take the monumental step of issuing a new Letters Patent to amend the decree his grandfather made in 1917. Such an act would be a deliberate, public repudiation, saying something that cannot be mistaken about their place in the family, or lack thereof. There is no document like this yet.
