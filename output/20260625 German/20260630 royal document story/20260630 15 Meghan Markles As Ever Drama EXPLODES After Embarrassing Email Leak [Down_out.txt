THE MATCHBOX AND THE MONARCHY: INSIDE THE DISASTROUS LAUNCH THREATENING MEGHAN'S EMPIRE

It was a single, deliberately placed photograph that set the internet ablaze. A burned-out match, resting on a box of new ones, next to a candle that was failing at its one job—a promotional image that seemed designed to confirm every criticism the brand had ever faced. How did a simple box of matches become the symbol of a multi-million dollar lifestyle empire in crisis, and what does it reveal about the precarious future of the entire Sussex enterprise, an enterprise built on the promise of a new, more authentic life beyond the palace walls?

The announcement on May 28th was meant to be a moment of quiet, curated luxury. The social media page for As Ever, Meghan Markle’s embattled lifestyle brand, posted just three words: “Arriving tomorrow.” The message was minimalist, confident, designed to stoke anticipation for the next chapter in a story that began with jam jars and royal connections. The following morning, the reveal landed not with a bang, but with the faint scratch of a matchstick. The new product was a limited-edition box of branded matches, not for sale individually, but included as a “complimentary item” within a four-candle “signature scent bundle” retailing for a formidable $256, or approximately £190.

In the hyper-competitive world of luxury goods, where perception is reality and every detail is scrutinized, the decision to elevate one of the most common, inexpensive, and disposable items in human history into an object of desire was a bold, if perplexing, move. Matches are a commodity, a functional afterthought purchased for cents at any supermarket checkout line. To frame them as a luxury “finishing touch” was to make a statement about the brand’s power to transform the mundane into the magnificent. But it was not the product itself that detonated a multi-day firestorm of criticism and mockery. It was the picture chosen to sell it. The image, a carefully composed still life, was a masterclass in unintentional self-sabotage. At its center was one of the brand’s signature candles, but it was not pristine. The flame had burned a narrow, deep crater straight down the middle, leaving thick, untouched walls of wax clinging to the glass—a classic, unforgivable sign of poor quality known to any candle aficionado as “tunneling.” Resting in the foreground, atop the fresh box of matches, was a single, spent match, its head blackened with carbon. A promotional photograph is the most deliberate form of communication a brand can produce. It is reviewed, edited, and approved. Someone, or a team of people, looked at this image—a portrait of product failure—and decided it was the perfect visual to entice a premium market. The internet, a court with no appeals process, delivered its verdict within hours. The image wasn't just a mistake; it was a confession.

### A FLAME, A TUNNEL, AND A QUESTION OF JUDGMENT

To understand the sheer velocity of the backlash, one must first understand the context into which this photograph was released. The As Ever brand was already under siege, fighting a defensive battle against a growing chorus of consumer complaints that centered on the very product featured in the disastrous image: its candles. For weeks, the narrative had been building, fueled by customer testimonials and reviews that painted a picture of a luxury product failing to deliver on its most basic promises. The most alarming of these were reports of candles arriving without a usable wick. One such claim came from a well-known reviewer with a substantial online following, instantly amplifying a single customer service issue into a viral indictment of the brand’s quality control. A second, similar report also circulated, creating a critical mass of doubt. The refrain, which quickly became a meme, was as brutal as it was memorable: “I’m wickless, and she’s witless.”

But even for customers who received a candle with a wick, a more insidious problem emerged: the burn pattern. The phenomenon of tunneling is the cardinal sin of high-end candle making. It occurs for specific, identifiable reasons—a wick that is too small for the diameter of the vessel, or a wax blend that fails to distribute heat evenly. In either case, it is a hallmark of poor design and cheap materials. A luxury candle, marketing experts will attest, sells an experience of perfection. Its value is derived from an even melt pool, a clean burn that consumes all the wax, and a fragrance that fills a room. Tunneling is the visual evidence that this promise has been broken. It leaves the customer with a significant portion of the expensive, scented wax they purchased permanently stuck to the sides of the jar, an unused and unusable monument to their wasted money.

For a brand positioning itself in the premium space, with individual candles priced at $64, such a flaw is not a minor imperfection; it is a fundamental breach of contract. When a consumer buys a $12 candle from a big-box store, their expectations are calibrated accordingly. When they invest in a $64 candle from a brand associated with a duchess, they are paying for an assurance of superior craftsmanship. The tunneling issue, therefore, landed with particular force. One critic, quoted in the British press, diagnosed the problem with brutal simplicity, suggesting the cause was the use of “very cheap and toxic paraffin wax” instead of the higher-quality soy or beeswax blends expected at that price point.

Compounding the frustration was the brand’s stringent “no returns” policy. In the world of e-commerce, a flexible return policy is the consumer’s safety net, an acknowledgment that products can have flaws. As Ever’s policy removed that net. Customers who received a defective, wickless, or tunneling candle found themselves with little recourse. Their only available outlet was the public square of the internet, where their complaints merged into a powerful, unified narrative of disappointment. This was the smoldering landscape into which the brand’s marketing team dropped its promotional photograph of a tunneled candle and a burned-out match. The image did not create the controversy; it became its official portrait. It was a visual confirmation of every negative review, a seemingly tone-deaf blunder that left observers to wonder if it was catastrophic incompetence or a bizarre, misjudged attempt at ironic self-awareness. The brand offered no clarification, leaving the image to speak for itself. And it spoke volumes.

### 'THAT'S SO OBNOXIOUS': THE $256 CONTRADICTION

Long before the first candle was poured or the first matchbox designed, a younger Meghan Markle, then an actress and lifestyle blogger, sat on a stage in Atlanta in 2016 and articulated a brand philosophy that would come back to haunt her. Speaking about her popular blog, *The Tig*, she drew a clear line in the sand, defining her ethos as one of accessible, relatable charm. “There are no $100 candles on my site,” she declared with a laugh. Then she delivered the fatal adjective: “That’s so obnoxious.”

In the annals of public relations missteps, this quote is a masterpiece of self-incrimination. The word she chose was not “expensive,” “unaffordable,” or “aspirational.” She chose “obnoxious.” It is a word that carries a moral, not just an economic, judgment. It implies that the act of selling such a product is not merely a business decision but a character flaw, a sign of pretension and a disconnect from ordinary people. It was a statement designed to position her as authentic, grounded, and fundamentally different from the purveyors of unattainable luxury.

A decade is a long time, and people, like brands, are allowed to evolve. The Meghan of 2016, building a personal blog, was operating in a different universe from the Duchess of Sussex of today, co-founder of a global media and lifestyle enterprise. A pivot in market positioning is a standard business practice. Yet, the internet never forgets, and the stark, mathematical hypocrisy of that 2016 statement juxtaposed with the 2025 price list of As Ever was an open invitation for ridicule. The signature scent collection, the very bundle that included the “complimentary” matches, retailed for $256. Even the individual candles, at $64 each, were well on their way to the price point she had once condemned.

The resurfaced quote required no additional commentary. Critics simply placed the video clip alongside a screenshot of the As Ever website. The contrast was devastatingly self-explanatory. It fed directly into a pre-existing narrative that her critics have long advanced: that her carefully cultivated image of relatability and authenticity masks a deep-seated ambition for elite status and extreme wealth. The “obnoxious” quote was no longer just a comment about candles; it became, for many, a key to her entire psychological profile. It suggested a person who was willing to adopt the posture of the people’s princess when it suited her, only to embrace the very behavior she had publicly scorned once she had the platform to do so. It was a wound inflicted by her own words, a perfect, self-contained loop of hypocrisy that no amount of brand repositioning could ever fully close.

### FROM ORCHARD TO ASHES: A BRAND'S TROUBLED GENESIS

The matchbox debacle was not an isolated incident but the latest chapter in a brand story plagued by missteps from its very conception. The venture was originally announced under the name American Riviera Orchard, a lyrical, evocative title meant to conjure images of sun-drenched Montecito luxury. But the name, while poetic, immediately ran into the prosaic realities of intellectual property law. Reports emerged that the U.S. Trademark Office had raised red flags. The name was too closely tied to a specific geographic location—the “American Riviera” is a well-known nickname for the Santa Barbara coast—creating significant hurdles for securing a broad, defensible trademark. Further complications reportedly arose from its similarity to other existing brand names. The dream of American Riviera Orchard was stalled by bureaucratic and legal obstacles before a single product had been shipped.

The pivot to the new name, As Ever, was a pragmatic solution. It was shorter, more abstract, and legally cleaner. But the change signaled an early wobble, a sign that the initial grand strategy had not been fully thought through. The first major product launch under the new banner was a limited-edition strawberry jam. The strategy was textbook influencer marketing, a tactic perfected by luxury brands worldwide. Instead of a public launch, approximately 50 jars were dispatched to a meticulously curated list of celebrities, influencers, and high-profile friends of the couple. The goal was to create an illusion of scarcity and overwhelming demand, to flood social media with aspirational images of Hollywood A-listers enjoying the Duchess’s jam, thereby generating a wave of organic-looking buzz before the product was available to the public.

The execution, however, fell conspicuously flat. While the distribution list itself generated media coverage—speculation over who made the cut and who was snubbed provided its own form of publicity—the anticipated deluge of enthusiastic social media posts never materialized. A handful of recipients posted polite thank-yous, but many of the 50 remained publicly silent. The campaign, designed to project an image of a brand at the center of the cultural conversation, instead projected an awkward silence. Online, jokes circulated about the jars being quietly relegated to the back of celebrity pantries. The derisive nickname “scam jam” began to trend, a label that, while not a factual claim of fraud, perfectly captured the public mood of skepticism. It reflected a growing sense that the brand was more style than substance, built on the fragile assumption that Meghan’s celebrity and royal status would automatically translate into commercial desirability. The jam rollout was the first real-world test of that assumption, and the results were, at best, inconclusive. It demonstrated that while her name could generate headlines, it could not necessarily compel her influential friends to act as her unpaid marketing team.

### SELLING A MEMORY, IGNITING A CONTROVERSY

Perhaps no single product in the As Ever line illustrates the brand’s unique blend of personal narrative and commercial ambition better than the Number 519 candle. The name is a direct reference to May 19th, the date of Meghan and Harry’s spectacular royal wedding at St. George’s Chapel in Windsor. The marketing copy describes the scent as an evocation of that day, promising notes of sunshine, joy, and the emotion of the historic occasion. It is an attempt to commodify a memory, to sell customers a $64 olfactory snapshot of a modern fairy tale.

But in choosing to monetize this specific moment, the brand waded directly into a pre-existing and contentious narrative. For years, a persistent story has circulated in the royal press regarding the wedding preparations. Multiple accounts, detailed in books and articles, have described an episode where Meghan allegedly expressed concerns about the scent of the 14th-century chapel, a place steeped in 500 years of royal history. According to these reports, a discussion was had about introducing additional air fresheners or diffusers before the guests arrived, a suggestion that was said to have been met with some resistance by palace staff, who considered the historic chapel’s natural ambiance to be part of its solemn character. The story gained further traction when the luxury fragrance brand Diptyque later confirmed its products were used as part of the preparations, lending a verifiable detail to the swirl of insider gossip.

Whether the story is entirely accurate or embellished over time is, in some ways, irrelevant. What matters is that it has become a fixed part of the Sussex lore, a widely-known anecdote among the very audience of royal watchers and cultural commentators most likely to be following her brand launch. Therefore, the Number 519 candle did not launch into a neutral market. It launched into a conversation already primed with a narrative of tension, of a clash between Hollywood modernity and ancient tradition. For critics, selling a candle meant to capture the “joyful atmosphere” of a morning that was also the subject of stories about disagreements over its smell felt deeply ironic, if not provocatively tone-deaf. It was another example of the brand seeming to operate in a vacuum, either unaware of or indifferent to the public perception and baggage attached to the very stories it was trying to sell.

### THE NETFLIX EQUATION AND THE ILLUSION OF DEMAND

Underpinning the entire As Ever venture is a powerful financial imperative. When Harry and Meghan stepped back from their roles as senior working royals, their stated goal was to achieve financial independence. The cornerstone of this new life was a massive, multi-year production deal with Netflix, signed in 2020 and reported to be worth a staggering sum. This deal was meant to provide the financial bedrock for their American dream. However, over the subsequent years, the nature of that relationship appeared to shift. Reports began to describe the arrangement as having evolved into a more limited “first-look” deal, a significant downgrade in industry terms. More recently, further reports claimed that Netflix had exited its involvement with the consumer product side of the Sussex business altogether.

While neither party has publicly confirmed the precise details of these changes, the perception of a cooling relationship with their most important financial partner has created a sense of heightened pressure on their other commercial ventures. As Ever is not just a passion project; it is a critical component of their financial strategy, a necessary revenue stream in their post-palace life. This context makes the brand’s marketing claims and sales performance a subject of intense scrutiny. The company has frequently announced that its products have “sold out,” creating a narrative of overwhelming success and insatiable demand.

Yet, the decision to include a complimentary box of matches with a $256 candle bundle introduces a logical inconsistency that has not gone unnoticed by skeptical observers. The gift-with-purchase is a classic retail strategy employed to incentivize a sale. It adds perceived value to convince a hesitant customer to complete a transaction. As any marketing analyst would confirm, this tactic is most effective when a purchase needs encouraging. If a product is genuinely and consistently selling out, with demand dramatically exceeding supply, there is no logical reason to add an incentive. The product’s scarcity is the incentive. The two signals—claims of constant sellouts and the use of promotional add-ons—do not sit comfortably together. This contradiction, combined with a reported website glitch that briefly seemed to expose inventory data suggesting sales were lower than implied, has fueled a persistent line of questioning about the true state of the business. The matchbox, intended as a luxury flourish, instead became another piece of evidence for those who believe the brand’s success story is more marketing spin than market reality.

### THE AUTHENTICITY PARADOX

In an interview with Harper’s Bazaar, Meghan offered a glimpse into her personal philosophy, a statement that now reads as the central, unresolved paradox of her public life and commercial endeavors. “The moment that you start making all of your personal decisions based on external judgment,” she said, “then you lose your authenticity.” It is a noble sentiment, a defense of individual integrity against the pressures of public opinion. Yet, it is a philosophy fundamentally at odds with the very business she has chosen to build.

A lifestyle brand, particularly in the luxury sector, is built on nothing *but* external judgment. Its success is entirely dependent on the careful management of perception. The curated photographs, the aspirational price points, the meticulously crafted brand story—every element is designed to influence how an audience sees, feels, and responds. External judgment is not a threat to a lifestyle brand; it is its lifeblood. The decision to buy a $64 As Ever candle instead of its $12 supermarket equivalent is a pure exercise in external perception, a purchase driven by the story, the status, and the identity the brand projects. The tension between her stated ideal of ignoring external judgment and her business model that relies on it entirely is a chasm that critics have gleefully explored.

This same tension was evident in a Bloomberg interview where she described having to wear nude tights during her time as a working royal as feeling “inauthentic.” It was a specific, relatable detail meant to illustrate the constraints she felt within the monarchy. But when placed alongside her current brand—with its royal title usage, its premium pricing, and its carefully constructed image of elite living—the example loses some of its power. For many observers, the journey from complaining about the inauthenticity of pantyhose to selling a $256 candle bundle named after her royal wedding represents not a journey toward authenticity, but away from it.

The humble matchbox did not create these contradictions. It did not invent the quality control issues, author the “obnoxious” quote, or generate the controversy around the wedding chapel’s scent. What it did, with devastating efficiency, was provide a single, photogenic object that gathered all these disparate threads of criticism into one undeniable image. The burned match, the tunneled candle, the luxury branding of a disposable good—it was a perfect, concise summary of the entire conversation. It symbolized a brand that seemed to be burning through its own potential, leaving a hollowed-out core. This is not a scrappy startup learning the ropes. This is a venture backed by a global celebrity with access to the best marketing minds and brand strategists money can buy. The expectations are, therefore, justifiably high. And with every misstep, from a jar of jam to a box of matches, the question grows louder: Is this an empire in the making, or just a slow, cinematic burn?
