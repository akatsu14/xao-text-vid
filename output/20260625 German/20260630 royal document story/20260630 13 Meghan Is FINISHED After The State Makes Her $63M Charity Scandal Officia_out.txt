THE ARCHEWELL PAPERS: HOW PUBLIC FILINGS REVEAL A CHARITY'S DEMISE AND A DUCHESS'S CALCULATED ESCAPE

On May 3rd, 2024, the State of California’s Department of Justice officially declared the Archewell Foundation, Prince Harry and Meghan Markle's global charity, a delinquent entity. The legal order to immediately cease all fundraising and spending came just seven weeks after Meghan launched her glamorous new lifestyle brand, American Riviera Orchard, to a storm of international media attention. The official documents, hidden in plain sight, reveal a story not of a simple clerical error, but of a deliberate pattern of priorities—and expose who was quietly building a commercial escape route while the architecture of their public service mission crumbled from within.

### A BOARDROOM FOR TWO

To understand the unraveling of the Archewell Foundation, one must first enter its boardroom. It is not a grand chamber filled with seasoned philanthropists and independent financial minds, the kind of sober oversight that guides multi-million-dollar non-profits. Instead, the entire governance structure, the complete legal authority responsible for stewarding millions in public donations, consisted of just two people: Meghan Markle and Prince Harry. This is not a tabloid rumor; it is a fact stated in their own filings. They were the founders, the public faces, and the final arbiters of every decision, with no independent voice at the table legally empowered to challenge them.

Charity Watch, the independent watchdog organization whose entire professional function is to evaluate the health and integrity of non-profits, identified this structure not merely as a weakness, but as a fundamental violation of established best practices. In a formal statement, the group noted that the foundation’s two-member board “falls well short of widely accepted nonprofit governance best practices.” This sterile, professional language masks a much harsher reality. In the world of non-profit governance, a two-person board composed of a married couple is not just a red flag; it is a bonfire. It creates a closed loop, an echo chamber where the founders’ personal and financial interests are structurally inseparable from the charity’s mission.

Consider the practical implications. When a proposal is made to divert the organization’s focus, resources, and energy towards the launch of a private, for-profit commercial brand for several months, who is there to object? In Archewell’s case, the only other voting member is the proposer's spouse, a resident of the same household, and a co-participant in the same overarching financial ecosystem. There is no independent trustee to stand for the donors, no external governor to ask whether this serves the stated charitable cause, no one to question the blurring of lines between philanthropic duty and commercial ambition. The system is designed to prevent dissent. It is structurally impossible for the foundation to overrule its founders, because the founders *are* the foundation.

For decades, the non-profit sector has learned a painful lesson, often through high-profile scandals, that unchecked power, even when wielded by well-intentioned founders, is a gateway to mission drift and financial mismanagement. Independent boards were not invented as a bureaucratic formality; they were forged in the fire of collapsed charities and betrayed donor trust. They exist to protect an organization from its own leadership, to ensure that the mission endures beyond the personalities of its creators. By forgoing this fundamental safeguard, the Duke and Duchess of Sussex built an organization that was, from its inception, vulnerable to precisely the kind of crisis that would eventually consume it. When the donations were pouring in and the global goodwill was at its peak, this structural flaw was a latent risk. But when the financial tides turned, it became the mechanism that allowed every other problem to fester, unchecked and unchallenged from within.

### THE THIRTEEN-MILLION-DOLLAR COLLAPSE

The financial trajectory of the Archewell Foundation reads like a case study in reputational implosion. The numbers, drawn directly from their own public filings, do not depict a gentle decline or a predictable market correction; they show a catastrophic collapse in donor confidence. In 2021, riding a global wave of sympathy following their explosive interview with Oprah Winfrey, the foundation raised an astonishing $13 million. This was the high-water mark, a moment when their narrative of escape and survival resonated with millions, who opened their wallets to support what they believed was a new, powerful force for good in the world.

Then came 2022. In a single year, donations plummeted from $13 million to just $2 million. This was not a gradual cooling of interest. A drop of over 84% in one fiscal cycle is a signal of active rejection. It suggests that the very same donors who had been so inspired in 2021 took a hard look at the foundation's activities, its impact, and its leadership, and made a conscious decision to walk away. The goodwill, it turned out, was a finite resource, and it had been spent. The decline continued, with the foundation raising a combined total of roughly $9.5 million over the next three years—less than it had raised in its single peak year.

The question that regulators and donors alike began to ask was stark: where was the money going? The 2023 filings offered a troubling glimpse. That year, Archewell raised over $5 million. Of that amount, only approximately $1.3 million was granted directly to charitable causes. The remaining $4 million—nearly 80% of the funds raised—was consumed by internal costs. Salaries, consultants, travel, and operational overhead absorbed the vast majority of donor contributions. This ratio is profoundly problematic for any charitable organization. While all non-profits have administrative costs, a healthy organization strives to maximize the percentage of funds that reach the front lines of its mission. An 80% overhead rate raises serious questions about efficiency and purpose.

Media analyst Brian Glicklich, providing a professional assessment on the record, did not mince words. He described the foundation as a “self-serving publicity mechanism for the couple and a means of platforming other money-making ventures.” He specifically highlighted the couple’s trips to Nigeria and Colombia as activities funded under the charity's operational umbrella. This assessment cuts to the heart of the controversy: was Archewell a genuine philanthropic vehicle, or was it, as Glicklich asserted, a platform? A platform uses the veneer of charity to generate positive press, maintain public relevance, and create opportunities for its founders—a perfectly legal, if ethically dubious, strategy. The financial data, showing a small fraction of funds reaching external causes, lent significant weight to this interpretation. The public, it seemed, had reached a similar conclusion long before the analysts. The collapse from $13 million to $2 million was not just a financial event; it was a public verdict.

### THE AUDIT THAT NEVER WAS

In the world of financial accountability, few documents carry more weight than an independent audit. It is the gold standard of transparency, a verification by a neutral, third-party accounting firm that an organization’s books are clean and its financial statements are accurate. It is the ultimate proof to donors and regulators that money is being handled responsibly. On its federal tax form, the 990—a legal document signed under penalty of perjury—the Archewell Foundation checked the box certifying that its finances were, in fact, subject to such an independent audit. This was not a promise or a goal; it was a sworn statement of existing fact.

Armed with this public declaration, Charity Watch did exactly what its mandate requires: it asked to see the audit. The first formal written request was sent on January 22nd, 2024. There was no response. A second letter followed on December 6th, 2024. Again, silence. A third and final request was made on January 6th, 2026, completing a two-year cycle of inquiries. The result was the same: no audit was ever produced.

This sustained, two-year refusal to provide a document that the foundation had legally certified as existing presents only two logical possibilities. The first is that a pristine, comprehensive audit does exist, sitting in a filing cabinet in Montecito, and that Archewell’s leadership simply chose to ignore three separate requests from America’s leading charity watchdog for reasons they have never explained. This scenario defies all logic. An organization reeling from a delinquency notice, a collapsing donor base, and public questions about its financial management would not conceal a clean audit. Producing it would be the single most effective way to restore credibility and silence critics. The act of sharing it would cost nothing and would immediately signal a commitment to transparency.

The second, and far more plausible, explanation is that the audit, in the form certified on the tax filing, does not exist or contains findings so problematic that the foundation’s leadership calculated that stonewalling was a less damaging option than disclosure. This is the conclusion that any regulator or seasoned investigator would inevitably draw from the documented pattern of behavior. The failure to produce the audit is not a mere administrative oversight; it is a profound breach of trust. It transforms the questions about financial management from speculation into a matter of documented non-compliance.

Ultimately, Charity Watch rendered its own verdict. Unable to obtain the most basic document required for a credible assessment, the watchdog concluded that Archewell “does not meet transparency benchmarks” and was therefore unable to assign it a rating. An organization that certifies the existence of a key accountability document on a legal filing and then refuses to produce it for two years is not suffering from a transparency gap. It is executing a transparency strategy—a strategy of deliberate obstruction designed to keep its internal financial workings shielded from public and regulatory scrutiny. This act alone would be enough to trigger serious concern from a state Attorney General’s office. But it was only one piece of a much larger, more troubling puzzle.

### A TALE OF TWO TIMELINES

The most damning evidence in the Archewell saga is not found in any single document, but in the glaring juxtaposition of two parallel timelines. It is a story told by the calendar, a split-screen narrative of where the household’s operational energy, meticulous planning, and professional focus were truly directed.

On the left side of the screen: March 14th, 2024. Meghan Markle launches American Riviera Orchard. The rollout is a masterclass in modern brand building. A soft-focus, black-and-white video appears on Instagram, showing Meghan arranging flowers and stirring a pot in a sun-drenched kitchen. The aesthetic is curated, aspirational, and deeply personal. The brand, described as an “extension of herself,” promises a return to the lifestyle content that made her famous on her blog, *The Tig*. Products like jams and honeys are sent to a hand-picked list of influencers, generating a tidal wave of free press. The initial product drop sells out almost instantly. Every detail is executed with precision.

Now, on the right side of the screen: May 3rd, 2024, just seven weeks later. The Attorney General of California declares the Archewell Foundation delinquent. The official reason cited is a failure to submit required annual reports and renewal fees. While the Sussexes’ team would later claim a check was lost in the mail, state regulators do not typically take the drastic step of suspending a charity’s license to operate over a single missing envelope. Such an action is almost always the culmination of a pattern of missed deadlines and incomplete paperwork—a symptom of a deeper, systemic neglect. The delinquency notice was a public declaration that the basic administrative duties of running a multi-million-dollar charity were not being met.

The pattern did not stop there. The compliance failures became chronic. On May 15th, 2025, the foundation’s registration with the state of California lapsed for a second time in two years, indicating that the most recent filings on record only covered the period through the end of 2023. Two separate, documented compliance failures in as many years.

Meanwhile, the American Riviera Orchard brand never missed a product drop. Its Instagram presence remained flawless. Its commercial operations continued without a single public hiccup. When one entity in a household repeatedly fails to file basic legal paperwork while the other entity executes a flawless commercial launch, the pattern ceases to be a coincidence. It becomes irrefutable evidence of where the attention lies. One project was being run like a serious, focused, and ambitious business. The other was being run like an afterthought, its legal obligations neglected until the state was forced to intervene. The dates on the documents, stark and emotionless, tell the whole story. The charity was taking every regulatory hit while the brand was collecting every commercial win.

### THE LAST LOYALIST WALKS AWAY

Documents and financial statements reveal the "what," but the human element often reveals the "why." In December 2025, as the foundation was being quietly dismantled, James Holt, one of the Sussexes’ longest-serving and most loyal aides, packed his bags and returned to the United Kingdom. His departure was not announced with a dramatic press release or a tell-all interview. It was a quiet exit, and in its silence, it spoke volumes.

Holt was not just another staff member in a famously high-turnover operation. He was an original, a day-one believer. He was there at Windsor Castle on the wedding day in May 2018, managing the complex logistics of a global media event. When Harry and Meghan made the earth-shattering decision to leave royal life in 2020, Holt made a decision of his own: he went with them. He crossed an ocean, leaving behind the established career path of a palace courtier for the uncertain future of their California project. He was instrumental in navigating the aftermath of the Oprah interview, the single event that redefined their public identity. He helped build and run Archewell through years of relentless media attacks and internal turmoil.

Insiders described Holt as perhaps the last remaining aide who had firsthand knowledge of the couple's experiences inside the palace—not from reading about it in a memoir, but from having been physically present. He stayed for seven years, a lifetime in the volatile world of the Sussex operation. He endured the fallout from Oprah, the Netflix series, and the memoir *Spare*. He witnessed the constant churn of staff, a phenomenon one royal source bluntly described by saying, "They churned through staff faster than normal people churn through toilet paper." By some counts, more than twenty senior aides have cycled through the Sussexes’ employment since their wedding.

Yet Holt stayed. Until December 2025. At the very moment the foundation’s $3 million deficit was a matter of public record, at the very moment the organization was cutting more than half its staff under the guise of a "restructure," the man who had been there through it all finally left. His departure, without public comment, can be interpreted as the ultimate vote of no confidence. It signaled that even for the most dedicated loyalist, a line had been crossed.

He was not alone. The same round of cuts saw the departure of the foundation's executive director and its director of programs and operations. This was not trimming administrative fat; this was the removal of the entire operational core. The person responsible for actually running the charitable programs, drawing a salary of $146,000, was let go in the same announcement that bizarrely framed the changes as an "expansion." The departure of seasoned professionals, especially one as steadfast as Holt, is a clear sign of profound institutional failure. It suggests that the mission they had all signed up for was no longer recognizable.

### AN EXPANSION INTO OBSCURITY

On December 19th, 2025, the final act of the Archewell Foundation was announced. It was not presented as a retreat or a downsizing, but as a bold new chapter. The Archewell Foundation, the press release declared, was "transitioning" to become Archewell Philanthropies. The language was aspirational and forward-looking, speaking of how the couple and their children would now be able to "expand upon their global philanthropic endeavors as a family."

The reality on the ground was the polar opposite of expansion. The announcement coincided with the staff cuts that gutted the organization. The donations had contracted by over 60% from the previous year. The $3 million deficit remained on the books. The audit requested by Charity Watch remained unproduced. The two California compliance failures were still on the public record. You do not expand an operation by firing more than half of your workforce and hemorrhaging cash. This was a strategic retreat, dressed in the warm, misleading language of growth.

The most significant detail, however, lay in the structural change itself. The new model Archewell was adopting, often known as fiscal sponsorship, typically carries far fewer mandatory public disclosure requirements than a standalone, independent foundation. The new entity would report less. Its financial picture would become less legible, its transactions less detailed, and its operations less available for public scrutiny.

This move is profoundly telling. At the precise moment that scrutiny of the foundation’s finances and governance was at an all-time high, its leadership chose to restructure into a form that is inherently less transparent. A regulator looking at this sequence of events—a delinquency notice, unanswered questions about a certified audit, a massive deficit, and then a pivot to a less transparent corporate structure—would not see a good-faith effort to reform. They would see a deliberate attempt to move the organization’s activities further into the shadows, away from the prying eyes of the public and the authorities.

The framing of the new entity around "family" was also a subtle but powerful rhetorical move. Family is private, personal, and operates by its own rules. The implication is that its philanthropic endeavors should be shielded from the kind of intrusive questions one might ask of a public-facing institution. But the money donated to the Archewell Foundation was not family money. It was public money, given in trust for specific charitable purposes. The donors who gave that money are legally and ethically entitled to a transparent accounting of its use. The pivot to a fiscal sponsorship model does not erase that obligation; it merely obscures it, making the paper trail harder to follow.

### THE PAPER TRAIL HAS NO AGENDA

When you pull back and view the entire timeline, the pattern is undeniable and requires no interpretive leap. The documents themselves, filed with state and federal authorities, tell the story.

March 2024: The commercial brand launches with flawless execution.
May 2024: The shared charity is declared delinquent by the state.
2024: The charity posts a $3 million deficit.
May 2025: The charity’s state registration lapses for a second time. The brand, meanwhile, continues its commercial operations on schedule.
December 2025: The charity is restructured into a less transparent form at the moment of peak financial and regulatory scrutiny, shedding the majority of its staff.

Through it all, the brand, American Riviera Orchard, remained pristine, operationally sound, and visually disconnected from the chaos engulfing the other side of the household’s public life. One of these entities was managed with the focus and discipline required for commercial success. The other was allowed to drift into regulatory non-compliance and financial insolvency. The reputation that serves as a form of financial collateral was being steadily eroded by the charity's failures. It is no surprise that in March 2026, reports emerged that a major Netflix investment in a related brand had been pulled. The reputational contagion had begun to affect the bottom line.

This is not a story pieced together from anonymous palace sources or hostile briefings. It is built from the ground up on a foundation of public records: the tax filings, the Charity Watch letters, the California delinquency notice, the on-the-record statements from figures like Dr. Sophie Chandauka, who chaired Harry’s other charity and accused his team of trying to turn it into an "extension of the Sussex PR machine."

Paperwork does not have an agenda. It does not give tearful interviews or harbor grudges against the monarchy. It simply records dates, numbers, and facts. In this case, the facts recorded in the documents show a clear and consistent pattern of where the operational priority was placed. While one dream was being carefully built, the other was being quietly abandoned. The dates do not lie.
