Royal wedding anniversaries that end in
divorce are not supposed to be marked at
all. This year, 13-year-old Prince
George marked one anyway. There is no
toast, no reissued photograph, and no
line in the court circular for a
marriage that ended the way this one
did. Charles and Diana were married at
St. Paul's Cathedral 45 years ago this
week.
Nobody at Kensington Palace had
scheduled a statement about it. The date
usually passes the way every anniversary
of that marriage has passed since 1996,
in silence, unmentioned, treated by the
family as a private matter that ended
badly and stayed that way.
Then, the evening before the
anniversary, George walked into the
Sunken Garden carrying something small
enough to close inside one hand.
Nobody in the press office approved it.
No photographer was there to record it.
Nobody watching the statue that week had
been told to expect anything at all. He
is Diana's eldest grandchild, born
almost 16 years after she died.
What he had brought that evening has a
wedding day history far older than
either of his parents, and how it
reached him, more than what it actually
was, is what turned one quiet visit into
a story the whole family is now talking
about. Here's what happened. This isn't
just a boy marking a date he had no
obligation to know about. It's the
family's future king at 13 choosing on
his own to fold his grandmother back
into the one royal wedding the palace
has spent almost 30 years declining to
mention. Charles and Diana's anniversary
has never been marked publicly since
their divorce, not with a statement, not
with so much as an old photograph
reissued for the day. If George's
gesture holds, this is the first time in
nearly three decades that anyone inside
Kensington Palace has treated 29th July
as a date worth remembering, rather than
one worth quietly letting pass. What he
did that evening in the Sunken garden is
one thing. What it did to his
grandfather's composure the following
morning once Charles found out is
another. And by every account, it did
not stay off his face for long.
The wedding itself needs little
introduction. 29th of July, 1981, St.
Paul's Cathedral.
What gets left out of the anniversary
retellings is smaller, and it is the
detail that matters here.
Since 1858, every royal bride has
carried a sprig of myrtle in her
bouquet, a tradition that began with
Princess Victoria, Queen Victoria's
eldest daughter, using cuttings from a
myrtle bush still growing today in the
grounds of Osborne House on the Isle of
Wight.
Diana's sprig in 1981 came from that
same original plant.
Custom holds that a cutting from each
bride's sprig is sent back to Osborne
afterward and replanted. So, the bush
that supplied Charles and Diana's
wedding is, in a sense, the same bush
that later supplied Catherine's in 2011.
Royal historian Hugo Vickers has written
that the myrtle tradition is one of the
few pieces of wedding ritual to survive,
largely unnoticed, into the modern era,
overshadowed by tiaras and headlines,
but unbroken since Victoria's day,
regardless of how any individual
marriage turned out.
The sunken garden at Kensington Palace,
where Diana's memorial statue has stood
since the summer of 2021, was replanted
before the statue's unveiling to reflect
the colors of Diana's own wardrobe,
rather than anything more generic.
By convention, the garden has no
connection to the myrtle tradition at
all, no ceremonial link, no precedent,
nothing written down anywhere that would
explain what happened next. That is the
gap a 13-year-old is reported to have
closed on his own the evening before his
grandparents would have marked 45 years
of a marriage that ended before he was
ever born.
There is no public confirmation of
how a sprig of wedding myrtle ended up
in a 13-year-old's hands 3 days before
an anniversary nobody was supposed to
mark.
But according to Hello magazine, which
first reported the detail, it did not
come from Osborne House at all. It came
from a cutting kept much closer, inside
Kensington Palace's own walled garden,
grown from stock descended from the
original plant, and normally reserved
for family weddings, not memorials.
Nobody has explained on the record how a
request for it reached the gardening
staff, or who signed off on cutting a
wedding flower for a garden that has
never, in 5 years, hosted one.
The request came anyway, and it was
granted. The next part is where a
gardening story stops being a gardening
story. A source close to the household
has said George's interest did not begin
with the anniversary at all. It began
weeks earlier, when he came across a
photograph of his grandmother on her
wedding day, and asked his father what
she had been holding.
William, according to the same account,
did not simply answer the question. He
is said to have arranged it himself,
involving no equerry, no private
secretary, and no press office, so that
his son could have a sprig of his own.
It was the heir to the throne
authorizing on his own initiative and
without telling anyone paid to manage
the family's image the first crack in
almost 30 years of official silence
about his parents' marriage. What
happened next has no official record,
only a witness.
2 days later, on the evening of 28th of
July, with no press notified and no
photographer present, George is reported
to have walked into the Sunken Garden
with a single protection officer, and
knelt at the base of his grandmother's
statue for less than a minute.
He did not leave the myrtle on the
plinth where flowers are normally left
for photographs. He is said to have
pressed it into the soil at the statue's
feet instead, low enough that a visitor
the next morning would need to know
exactly where to look to find it.
Then he stood, said nothing anyone
recorded, and left the way he came.
One person did know where to look, and
within days, by a route nobody at the
palace has explained, the detail reached
Hello magazine.
What George said, if he said anything at
all while he knelt there, has not been
repeated by anyone in a position to have
heard it.
What happened to his grandfather once
the detail reached him did not stay
private for nearly as long. Charles was
told the next morning before his
official engagements began.
According to a source who spoke to the
Daily Mail, he did not say much when he
heard it.
He asked one question about exactly
where at the statue's base the myrtle
had been left, and then said nothing
else on the subject for the rest of the
day.
What he did instead was clear one
appointment that had been on his diary
for weeks, and go to the sunken garden
himself, alone except for a single
protection officer, on the morning of
29th of July. That sounds small, a king
standing by a statue for a few minutes
before breakfast. It wasn't. Charles has
never been photographed at his former
wife's memorial since it was unveiled in
2021, not on an anniversary, not on her
birthday, not once in 5 years.
Camilla was not there. No statement was
issued. No court photographer had been
briefed because none had been told to
come.
The only record of the visit at all came
from staff who happened to be tending
the same garden that had supplied the
myrtle 3 days before, and who described
a man standing still for longer than the
visit strictly required, his hands in
his coat pockets, looking down at a
patch of soil rather than up at the
statue itself. This is where a marriage
the palace spent almost 30 years
declining to discuss finally gets
acknowledged in its own understated way
by the two people who lived it and the
one who never got the chance to.
William answered his son's question
about a photograph.
George turned that answer into a gesture
at a statue.
And Charles, the following morning,
appears to have gone to say something no
one recorded to a woman he married 45
years ago, divorced 15 years into it,
and lost roughly a year after that.
Nothing about it looks coordinated, and
that is precisely what makes it hold
together.
George's request started with a
photograph, not an instruction.
William's help started with a father
answering a question, not a plan drawn
up by anyone paid to manage the family's
image. Charles's visit came a full day
after the fact, once he already knew
what had happened without him, and once
there was nothing left for him to
arrange, only somewhere to stand.
Three people, a generation apart,
arriving separately at roughly the same
instinct, none of them waiting for the
other two to go first. What this
ordinary Wednesday actually did was
quietly restore a date the family had
let go dark for almost three decades.
Royal wedding anniversaries are not, as
a rule, occasions the palace marks once
a marriage has [music] ended, and this
one had ended twice over, first in
divorce, then in something nothing could
undo.
There was never an obligation for anyone
to remember 29th July at all. That a
13-year-old chose to anyway, without
being asked, says something about how
the next [music] reign may end up
holding Diana's memory.
Not through renamed gardens or official
portraits, but through small specific
gestures a child decides on his own are
worth doing.
It suggests [music] the family's
relationship with this particular date
was never really resolved by three
decades of silence.
It was only paused, waiting for someone
young enough not to know he wasn't
supposed to market. Diana chose a sprig
of myrtle for her own wedding at 20 from
a bush she likely never thought about
again.
45 years later, her grandson chose the
same flower from the same lineage for a
garden built to remember her instead.
