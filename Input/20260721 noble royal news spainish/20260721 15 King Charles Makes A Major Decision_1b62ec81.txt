Title of the “Ghost” in 1917

The only way to understand the strange muddled condition of identity that Lady Louise found herself in was to look back almost a century ago, to a world set aflame by the Great War. When 1917 came along the British monarchy was about to fade from sight. On the other side of the canal, not only were ancient European dynasties falling, they were being systematically dismantled by revolt and conflict. And Queen Elizabeth II’s grandfather, King George V, had watched his cousin, Tsar Nicholas II of Russia, overthrown violently in horror. His family’s fate was a harbinger of darker things to come. Another relative, Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany, was the public face of the adversary. He was a regular reminder of the monarchy’s Germanic roots, embodied in their very name: the House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha.

There was a growing republican feeling in the political air of Britain, with a strong anti-German feeling towards the German people. There was a general sense that the Crown appeared alien, obese and dangerously out of touch. George V was pragmatic and knew that to preserve his personal survival he would have to undertake a radical act of rebranding. He decided to alter his surname from the German to the very English “Windsor,” and then turned his attention to the size of his family. There were so many princes, princesses and tranquil highnesses that the sense grew in the general public that the nation was making an indulgence it could no longer afford.

His answer was in the form of a strong and durable royal act of legislation called the Letters Patent of 1917. Letters Patent are formal, open instruments of law issued under the full sovereign authority of the monarch. This was not just a memo from the palace. It was meant to be a severe, decisive trimming of the royal tree, and it was meant to be. The new law was blunt in its simplicity and the natural sexism it contained: the children of the king were automatically entitled to the title of Prince or Princess and the style of His or Her Royal Highness (HRH). The privilege was also available to the monarch’s grandchildren, but only if they were the children of the monarch’s sons. Children of a daughter of a monarch were not permitted to participate. It was a legal architecture designed for a patriarchal age, a firewall to contain the spread of titles and to present a more focused, leaner institution to a skeptical public. This decree has been in place for over a century and has been the one to determine who is and who isn’t deemed to be a member of the Royal Family.

And it is in this legal framework which has existed for a century, that the story of Lady Louise Windsor starts. When she was born on 8 November 2003 she was also known as Her Royal Highness Princess Louise of Wessex by the express words of George V's Letters Patent. She was born November 8, 2003, the daughter of Prince Edward, the youngest son of reigning queen Queen Elizabeth II. It was not something you could buy as a present or get as a position, but her legal birthright, given to her directly and immediately by law. From the moment she had taken her first breath, the label had been there, a ghost identity, ready to be recognized. The reason very few people ever call her this name is not because of the law, but because of a decision that was made before she was born, and that is a decision that is both purposeful and very meaningful.

A DISAPPOINTMENT AFTER DIANA

The decision to deprive Louise of her royal title was made in the heat of the most tragic modern chapter in the history of the monarchy. In June 1999, Prince Edward married Sophie Rhys-Jones, less than two years after the death of Princess Diana, and the Royal Family had yet not felt the aftermath. Her death had provoked national anguish, and this had given rise to a wave of popular rage directed against the institution itself. The institution was seen as cold, heartless and unforgiving, especially towards the “People’s Princess.”

Diana’s loss of her HRH status after her divorce from Prince Charles was a huge event, a major and emotionally charged emblem of this perceived cruelty. The stripping of “Her Royal Highness” was perceived by the public as a petty and cruel measure designed to remove her from the family she had reenergized. The title used to be just a formality but now had become a significant symbol of legitimacy and belonging. The whole concept of royal titles was contentious in view of the very sensitive context.

Buckingham Palace made a somewhat weird announcement about the impending wedding of the Queen's youngest son. The announcement comes against the backdrop of the aforementioned circumstances. A dukedom is a high rank in the British peerage that is typically bestowed on the child of a sovereign on the occasion of their marriage. But Edward was to be made Earl of Wessex. Even more unexpected was a statement from the Palace that any children born of the union would not be styled as princes or princesses. Or, they would take the courtesy titles of the Earl's children. In future, Viscount Severn would be a son, Lady a daughter.

It was a master stroke in public relations and a planned move to defuse the tensions of that age. It was a quiet indicator that this new royal couple would not be like the others. They would be no welcome addition to the ranks of publicly funded royals who are household names. Their children would be raised at the margins, shielded from the duties and privileges that, in the collective imagination, had resulted in Diana’s early death. The modern counterpart of a bargain was a life of relative solitude in return for a lesser involvement in public life.

On the other hand, this is the critical and sometimes misinterpreted detail. Buckingham Palace has put out an official press statement but it does not overturn Letters Patent given by a previous sovereign. The 1999 statement then went on to outline how the children would be “styled,” a convention and personal taste. Legally it didn’t and can’t take away the rights that were given to them by the order that was issued in 1917. The Princess title and the HRH designation were not removed, but were suspended and left inactive for the time being.

This important distinction, Sophie, now Duchess of Edinburgh, came to the conclusion, a few years later, was correct. She was very clear about the subject, speaking honestly and openly in an interview with The Sunday Times in 2020. She affirmed that Louise and her younger brother James are legally entitled to keep their HRH titles. In simple words the family had decided not to use them and this was because they wanted their children to have a more solid start to their lives. Crucially, Sophie said the option being considered would not be forever. Her statement said Louise would be old enough to decide for herself when she turned eighteen. She might remain Lady Louise, or she could finally take the title that had been hers all along. Louise was born in November 2003, therefore she turned 18 in November 2021. She did not make any public adjustments yet the choice did not become invalid. A powerful and consequential decision is still to be taken, and is still waiting to be made.

The previous paradigm allowed even distant relatives of the king to expect a life of ceremonial duties paid by royal grant, and this was a significant departure from the previous scenario. It was a silent acknowledgment that the institution was changing, that the gilded cage was being dismantled in favor of a life that was not only freer but more personal responsibility for those not in the straight line of succession.

THE KING'S GAMBLE, THE CRISIS OF A MONARCHY

While Louise was being raised to be self-sufficient in her professional life, the organization she is a part of was on the cusp of a crisis that her parents could not reasonably have anticipated. King Charles III came to the throne with a very clear, long-standing mandate: to create a “slimmed-down monarchy.” He has long been an advocate of the idea of a more focused, cost-effective and efficient Firm, with fewer working royals on the public payroll. It was a smart and straightforward answer to years of the media criticizing the family as too large, too costly, and filled with a cast of characters sometimes described in unflattering terms as "hangers-on." On paper, the strategy was sound. The truth is that it has collided with the hard realities of biology and controversy, and has left a fatal vulnerability at the heart of his reign.

Then this fragile construction got a disastrous one-two blow. The Palace said at the start of 2024 that King Charles was being treated for cancer. Two of the four senior working royals under the age of seventy were faced with serious health challenges with little warning, which meant they had to cut back dramatically on the amount of public duties they were undertaking. Royal specialists and constitutional pundits have been raising the alarm with growing urgency: the model that is currently in place is simply not viable. The monarchy carries out thousands of public engagements every year and is patron of hundreds of charities, military regiments and non-governmental organizations. Many of the patronages held by the late Queen Elizabeth and Prince Philip are now vacant, leaving institutions with historic ties to the royal family virtually orphaned. The monarchy has been diminished in size and is now in risk of becoming invisible because it cannot perform the responsibilities of public service and national recognition that are essential for its future existence.

For a royal family that has often struggled to avoid the taint of controversy and allegations of extravagance, maintaining unquestionable credibility is paramount. What was most remarkable about her story was never the title she decided not to use; rather it was the bedrock of character she was building in its absence, strong enough to bear the Crown itself.
